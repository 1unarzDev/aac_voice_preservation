\begin{abstract}
Some people who cannot speak compose a message from a few keywords, and a language model expands them into a sentence (augmentative and alternative communication, AAC).
The keywords \kw{supervisor read} can lose a \say{not}, and catching that needs an oracle: an automated checker that decides pass or fail and is itself software that can be wrong.
We built a small test harness for such systems and then tested its oracle.
Two defects in our own harness, each traced to a regression test, show why.
First, relaxing a rule to remove a false alarm made the oracle accept a changed sentence.
\say{You borrowed my car this weekend} passed as the acceptance \say{Yes, you can borrow my car}, and the relaxed rule detected only \kof{or_acc_mut_det} keyed acceptance mutants.
Second, a bug in our input builder showed models chips that contradicted the source message in \RES{wb_conflicts} of \RES{wb_msgs_restored} inputs; a validator now rejects them before generation.
On \RES{hw_nkept} human-written pairs from \RES{hw_nwriters} writers, four models read bare keywords as affirmative in \pct{hwaff} of outputs, which is an Assumption by design because bare keywords carry no negation.
A speech-act chip, a bracketed tag, raised the intended speech act by \RES{hwp1} percentage points (pp) over a length-matched word.
This is a manipulation check: on a pre-specified machine-written confirmation set the speech act was kept in only \pct{wcm_act_chip1} of targets even with the chip.
Plain typed requests gained a politeness marker in \pct{hwpzadd} of outputs against \pct{hwpzrem} removed (net \RES{hwp2}\,pp), driven by Gemma-3-4B (net \RES{hwpz_gemma}\,pp).
On the confirmation set all three contrasts have the same sign and intervals above zero, but the net politeness effect is small (+\RES{wcp2}\,pp \ci{wcp2}).
The oracle's labels agreed with trained human coders at $\kappa=\RES{jve_kappa}$ and $\RES{jvh_kappa}$ on critical labels in two sets of outputs; verdict-level $\kappa$ was \RES{jvh_vk_min}--\RES{jvh_vk_max} on the human-written pairs.
No AAC users took part, and the harness is for developers.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%% =====================================================================
Some people who use augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) compose a message from a few keywords, because each selection costs time and effort~\cite{valencia2023less,cai2024speakfaster}.
A language model then expands the keywords into a sentence~\cite{shen2022kwickchat,cai2024speakfaster}.
Typing \kw{supervisor read} might mean \say{The supervisor read} or \say{The supervisor did not read} (Fig.~\ref{fig:gallery}).
Whether the model erred depends on what the user supplied, and a text-similarity score cannot know that.
Symbol- and grid-based AAC is out of scope.

This paper asks which output changes count as failures, given exactly what the user supplied, and how to test the checker that decides.
The software-engineering problem is the oracle.
Deciding whether an output kept the meaning needs a checker, here an LLM that extracts a few explicit labels.
An unvalidated checker silently decides every result, so we treat testing the oracle as a first-class contribution beside testing the models.
We contribute:
(1)~a harness with five requirements R1--R5 that have stated sources and executable tests (\S\ref{sec:harness});
(2)~tests of the oracle, a fault-by-test matrix, and two defects we made ourselves, each traced from symptom to regression test (\S\ref{sec:oracle});
(3)~evidence on human-written pairs and on a machine-written confirmation set that the tests find real, model-specific behavior (\S\ref{sec:results}); and
(4)~the harness and stored outputs as an artifact.
The harness is for developers, not for assessing AAC users or their messages.

\section{Requirements and Harness}
\label{sec:harness}
%% =====================================================================
\paragraph*{Families and channels}
A \emph{family} is a minimal pair of source messages that share content words and differ on one \emph{critical} dimension: polarity, agent (who acts), certainty, speech act (question, request or statement), politeness, or time/quantity.
Each variant is rendered through five \emph{channels}, which say how much of the message the user supplied: \emph{bare} keywords; \emph{+word}, which adds one noncritical content word; \emph{+chip}, which adds the critical value as a chip such as \chip{NOT}; \emph{+all}, with every chip the variant needs; and \emph{full}, the source sentence itself.
Every variant needs at least one chip, so the unmarked reading gets a counter-chip (e.g., \chip{PLAIN}).
A chip is a \emph{probe} of whether supplied information is used, not a proposed interface: its real cost depends on the access method (a dwell, a menu traversal, a grid button), and it adds a vocabulary to learn and an error-recovery path.

\paragraph*{Requirements}
For output $y$ from input $x$, let $L_d(y)$ be the extracted value on dimension $d$ and $C(x)$ the commitments that $x$ carries.
Table~\ref{tab:trace} states each requirement with its source and its test.
A \emph{Violation} means a supplied value was not kept (R1, R2); an \emph{Assumption} means the output committed to a value the input left open (R3).
A reference-fidelity score would charge the model for what the interface omitted, and a similarity score would pass an added \say{please} on a typed sentence.
All five requirements are our proposal, derived from the structure of the task and from defects we met; none was derived with AAC users or clinicians.
The declared policy behind R2, do not change typed wording unless the person asks for rewriting, protects authorship.
Some users prefer that a system rewrite their text, so R2 is a policy to test, not a recommendation.

\paragraph*{Harness}
Fig.~\ref{fig:harness} shows the pipeline.
A family specification is rendered into channels, and the R5 validator checks that each displayed chip matches the claim.
A generator produces outputs.
An extractor (gpt-6.1-sol, a different model from those under test) answers the family's critical question and five general questions from the output alone; added-content extraction also sees the visible input.
Two extractor prompts (J1, J2) differ in wording and option order, and critical disagreements go to a cross-family arbiter (claude-opus-5-5) or stay unknown.
Verdicts are then computed in code from labels, item specification and channel, so a rule change re-scores every stored output without model calls.
The oracle decides only whether a fixed set of explicit, text-level values is kept.
It does not judge fluency, referents or whether anyone would accept the sentence.

\paragraph*{Adding a chip: worked example and cost}
Suppose a developer wants a \chip{THANKS} chip for the politeness dimension.
The chip table in \texttt{src/labels.py} gets \RES{ec_new_entry_lines} new entry:
\begin{center}\footnotesize\texttt{"[THANKS]": ("politeness", frozenset(\{"thanks/apology"\}))}\end{center}
The verdict code (\texttt{src/attribute.py}, \RES{ec_loc_attribute} lines) and the R5 validator (\texttt{src/validate\_cue\_inputs.py}, \RES{ec_loc_validator} lines) both read that table, so neither changes.
In a dry run (\texttt{extend\_cost.py}) the validator then marks \kw{Keywords: [THANKS] extra pillow} compatible with a thanks/apology source and conflicting with a plain one, and re-scoring all \RES{ec_rescored_rows} stored human-written-pair output rows took \RES{ec_rescore_seconds}\,s with \RES{ec_model_calls} model calls.
A new dimension, not just a new chip, also needs a codebook option and an extractor question, which means new extractor calls; we did not measure that cost.
No developer other than the authors has used the harness.
Regenerating outputs needs the API models, whose versions may be retired and whose outputs are not bit-reproducible.

\paragraph*{Evidence sets}
All items are authored or crowd-written, not messages from AAC users.
The \emph{human-written pairs} (primary) are \RES{hw_nkept} pairs kept of \RES{hw_ncand} candidates, written by \RES{hw_nwriters} non-author writers over six dimensions and kept after three blind independent checks; \RES{hw_nfam} families are analysed.
The \emph{confirmation set} has \RES{wc_n_fam} fresh machine-written families on three dimensions, whose three contrasts were pre-specified in a git-timestamped plan frozen before generation.
The \emph{development set} has \RES{n_fam_test} authored families used to develop prompts and codebooks.
\emph{Corpus transfer} uses crowd-written fictional messages from AACText~\cite{vertanen2011imagination}, and the \emph{composition study} (R4) adds a second chip to \RES{wd_nscen} scenarios.
Four models run with one fixed one-sentence prompt (greedy): Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Gemma-3-4B-it, three open models run locally with 4-bit quantization, and one API model, gpt-6.1-terra.
All intervals are pointwise 95\% percentile intervals from writer-cluster (human-written pairs) or seed/construction-cluster (confirmation set) bootstraps, with 10,000 resamples over a fixed model roster.

\section{Results: Does the Harness Find Real Behavior?}
\label{sec:results}
%% =====================================================================
\subsection{Primary replication on human-written pairs}
Table~\ref{tab:primary} gives three contrasts.
\emph{Act-chip gain} is the speech-act chip minus a length-matched word.
\emph{Net politeness addition} is politeness added to plain typed sentences minus politeness removed from polite ones.
\emph{Affirmative default} is the share of bare polarity outputs that are affirmative.
The plan called them P1--P3 (unrelated to prompts).
They were pre-specified for the confirmation set; on the human-written pairs they are an exploratory replication and were not preregistered.
All hold under Holm correction, but each rests on only \RES{hw_wr_min}--\RES{hw_wr_max} writers.
We therefore claim direction and consistency, not interval width, because a writer-cluster percentile interval with so few clusters is probably too narrow.
The chip gain is positive on all six dimensions, between \RES{hw_gain_min} and \RES{hw_gain_max}\,pp, with every lower bound above \RES{hw_gain_lo_min}\,pp.
Collapsing near-duplicate pairs, excluding one writer, reversing the arbiter's judgment calls or using all candidates flips no decision; the largest shift is \RES{hw_shift_max}\,pp (\RES{hw_shift_all}\,pp with all candidates).
Because the chip text enters the model's prompt, Act-chip gain and Affirmative default are close to manipulation checks of whether the harness separates supplied from missing information, which is their purpose.
They bound what a chip can do rather than show how often systems fail.
The politeness figures use two readings, named once: \emph{added} is the share of plain sources that gained a marker, \emph{removed} is the share of polite sources that lost one, and \emph{net} is added minus removed.

\subsection{Confirmation set}
All three contrasts have the same sign on the confirmation set, and every interval excludes zero (Table~\ref{tab:primary}).
Act-chip gain and Affirmative default are of similar size to the human-written pairs.
Net politeness addition is small, with a lower bound of \RES{wcp2_lo}\,pp.
Confirmation is qualified.
First, \RES{wc_prefreeze} cached requests predate the final item freeze.
Second, Act-chip gain relies on post-hoc arbitration of extractor disagreements (\RES{wc_arb_rows} rows); with those labels masked as unknown it is \RES{wcp1_um}\,pp, a sensitivity analysis in the last row of Table~\ref{tab:primary}.
Net politeness addition and Affirmative default contain no arbitrated rows.
``Pre-specified and git-timestamped'' means the plan was committed to a private repository before generation, which is not independently verifiable.
A hash of the protocol was deposited publicly as GitHub release \texttt{\RES{hash_release}}; it post-dates the confirmation-set freeze and so does not certify it.

\subsection{Assumptions, full-input violations and model heterogeneity}
Table~\ref{tab:ladder} separates the two kinds of evidence.
In the italic cells the input is ambiguous, so the match is the model's default and is bounded by a 50\% design ceiling; it is not accuracy.
In the other cells the chip or sentence is visible, so the match measures preservation.
Under bare input, \kn{wcaff} directional polarity outputs on the confirmation set were affirmative; these are Assumptions, not failures, and the interface decides who pays an extra selection to say \say{not}.
That default may be an artifact of heavy compression, so we asked humans: \RES{el_nann} annotators typed keywords for \RES{el_nmsg} crowd-written AACText messages (\RES{el_nsets} sets).
On the \RES{el_neg_c_n} messages with a negation cue they dropped it in \pct{el_neg_h} of sets, against \pct{el_neg_c} for a deliberately crude four-word rule-based compressor.
So the Assumption channel is realistic but the compressor is not a user model.

\subsection{Composition (R4)}
\label{sec:comp}
The composition study adds a second chip to a single-chip input and counts pass$\to$fail transitions of the first chip's value, minus the disagreement between two greedy repeats of the identical input.
It has \RES{wd_nscen} scenarios, nine inputs each, two encodings of the speech-act chip (\chip{TELL} and its alias \chip{STATEMENT}), four models and two draws (\RES{wd_nreq} requests).
Failures depend on both model and encoding.
Qwen loses the agent in \kn{wdqa} transitions under \chip{TELL} (\RES{wdqa_d}\,pp above the repeat floor \ci{wdqa_d}) and in \RES{wdqa_stmt_k} under \chip{STATEMENT}.
Llama loses polarity in \kn{wdll} transitions under \chip{STATEMENT} (\RES{wdll_d}\,pp \ci{wdll_d}) and in \RES{wd_tellpol_llama_k} under \chip{TELL}.
The single-chip repeat floor is \RES{wdll_rep_k}/\RES{wdll_rep_n} for Llama.
An R4 test must therefore fix the encoding, because a failure that appears under one encoding of the same meaning is an encoding defect before it is a model property.
This study is exploratory and machine-scored.
Critical labels fell back to J1 on \RES{wd_j1_rows} of \RES{wd_rows} scored rows, and the Qwen \chip{TELL} examples expose a codebook-rule ambiguity that agreement of the two prompts does not settle.

\section{Testing the Oracle}
\label{sec:oracle}
%% =====================================================================
Every result above is only as good as the checker, so we test it as software, in the spirit of the test-oracle literature~\cite{weyuker1982nontestable,barr2015oracle} and of mutation analysis~\cite{jia2011mutation}: an authorized change must pass and an unauthorized one must fail.
\emph{Identity test:} an unchanged source injected as its own full-channel output must pass.
\emph{Scoped mutants:} a hand-written one-value change to a development-set source must be detected.
\emph{Keyed challenge:} hand-written controls and one-feature mutants, with keys hashed before the first extractor call, must be classified as keyed.
\emph{Human gate:} extractor labels against human coders.

\paragraph*{Two defects, two regression tests}
Table~\ref{tab:trace2} traces the two defects we made while building the harness; neither was seeded.
In Defect A, the identity test failed \RES{id_fail_raw} of \RES{id_n} reproductions, all one sentence, \say{Yes, you can borrow my car this weekend.}, coded \emph{\RES{id_fail_got}} against the target \emph{\RES{id_fail_want}}.
The model had done its job; the checker demanded a distinction the text need not force.
The obvious repair, accepting \emph{statement} for an acceptance target, passed identity but failed the next test.
The relaxed oracle detected only \kof{or_acc_mut_det} acceptance mutants and missed both statement substitutions (\say{You borrowed my car this weekend.}), whereas without the relaxation it detected \kof{or_new_mut_det_raw} mutants across groups.
Removing a false alarm removed fault sensitivity.
Both refusal/acceptance families are therefore quarantined from every primary result (leaving \RES{n_fam_primary} of \RES{n_fam_test} development families) until a predicate passes both tests.
In Defect B, an audit of \RES{wb_msgs_restored} restored-chip inputs built from AACText messages found \RES{wb_conflicts} whose displayed chip contradicted the stored source, e.g., \chip{MAYBE} for a plain assertion, after a silent fallback in our builder.
In the \RES{wb_maybe_msgs} \chip{MAYBE} messages, all \RES{wb_maybe_n} outputs followed the visible chip and failed the source, which a source-fidelity score counts as model failures.
The R5 validator decodes only the rendered input and now rejects all \RES{r5_conf} before generation.
It matches the audit on \RES{r5_agree}/\RES{r5_n} and never certifies the \RES{r5_sure} coarser \chip{SURE} restorations.

\paragraph*{Human gate}
The first human gate, with two annotators, was run on the development items (the extraction inventory used to develop the codebook), with plurality references and tied items excluded rather than adjudicated.
Its pre-specified criterion ($\kappa\geq0.6$) passes on all \RES{human_gate_n} fields.
It validates extraction of explicit markers on those items, not repaired text or corpus-transfer readings.
Two later checks use outputs the harness had not been developed on: outputs for human-elicited keywords and outputs for human-written pairs (Table~\ref{tab:oracle}).
The excluded items are \RES{jve_excl} of \RES{jve_items} and \RES{jvh_excl} of \RES{jvh_items}.
Those checks are the oracle's evidence on the headline-type data, though their coders were also taught the codebook, and the verdict $\kappa$ values on human-written pairs rest on small counts of positive verdicts.
Machine agreement is not validation: on \say{I can't wait to go to the party.}, both extractor prompts, the rule-based extractor and two further machine coders answered \emph{negated}, and both human annotators answered \emph{affirmative}, as keyed.

\section{Related Work}
%% =====================================================================
\paragraph*{Test oracles, metamorphic testing and requirements for ML}
Whether an output is correct is the oracle problem~\cite{barr2015oracle}; for programs without a practical oracle, partial or pseudo oracles are standard~\cite{weyuker1982nontestable}, and metamorphic testing checks relations between runs instead~\cite{chen2018metamorphic}.
Mutation analysis measures a test suite by whether it detects seeded faults~\cite{jia2011mutation}; we apply that idea to the oracle, not the system.
Requirements engineering for ML stresses that data-dependent behavior needs explicit, testable requirements~\cite{vogelsang2019re4ml}; our R1--R5 are one such set, with sources and tests stated.

\paragraph*{Testing LLM systems}
CheckList organizes behavioral tests into minimum-functionality, invariance and directional tests~\cite{ribeiro2020checklist}; Cho et al.\ catalogue metamorphic relations for LLM-based NLP~\cite{cho2025metamorphic}; COLLIE tests compositional constraints~\cite{yao2024collie}; Sensei combines a user simulator with assertions and metamorphic relations~\cite{delara2026sensei}.
We claim no new testing technique.
LLM judges show position, verbosity and self-preference biases~\cite{zheng2023judging,panickssery2024selfpreference}, and similarity metrics can miss negation~\cite{koch2022selfeval}.
Pauli et al.\ show that preservation metrics look valid when test data confound style change with content errors~\cite{pauli2025stylegap}; our identity and mutant tests apply that idea to a checker.

\paragraph*{Preservation and LLMs in AAC}
Qu et al.\ evaluate speech-act retention in style transfer~\cite{qu2023conditioning}; rewriting can keep semantic similarity while erasing pragmatic markers~\cite{navneet2026voice}; and LLMs favor particular politeness strategies~\cite{zhao2025politeness}.
AAC systems studied with users expose intent controls~\cite{valencia2023less,valencia2024compa,mao2025designprobes,xiang2025socializechat}; Lacosse et al.\ keep bracketed function words such as \say{not}~\cite{lacosse2026cognitivesteering}; Choudhury et al.\ test whether affect cues reach the output~\cite{choudhury2025alignment}; R-SPEAK scores faithfulness and was co-designed with people with aphasia~\cite{altamimi2026rspeak}.
We add a conditional specification of which commitments are testable under each input, and tests of the oracle that checks them.
Accessibility engineering spans the life cycle~\cite{paiva2021accessibility}, but conformance checks do not see whether generated text keeps a user's meaning.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}
%% =====================================================================
\emph{Construct.} Labels are coarse, explicit categories; \say{plain} means no explicit marker, and referents, negation scope and certainty degree are only partly covered.
Passing the checker is weaker than keeping the typed wording, and a scored repair is not necessarily a sentence someone could say.
\emph{Oracle.} All rates depend on an LLM extractor and arbiter; the arbiter ran after outcomes were known, and human coders were taught the codebook.
The first human gate's $\kappa$ is on development items, not on confirmation data.
The defect trace has two defects, both found by our own process; it shows that the process works, not that it finds defects we did not anticipate, and the harness has not been compared with a baseline oracle such as embedding similarity.
\emph{Internal.} The confirmation set has early cached requests and post-hoc arbitration; the human-written pairs are not preregistered and have few writers per contrast.
\emph{External.} Four models, three small and quantized; one prompt; English; constructed and crowd-written, not AAC-user, messages.
Model versions may be retired, and API outputs are not bit-reproducible.
\emph{Conclusion.} Effects are model-specific, and zero observed events do not establish zero risk.
Selection counts omit reading, navigation and fatigue~\cite{frisch2026complicated}.

\section{Conclusion}
%% =====================================================================
An oracle for meaning preservation is software and needs tests of its own.
Ours caught a repair that hid real substitutions and a builder that supplied the wrong chip, and its labels agree with trained human coders on two sets of outputs.
With it, human-written pairs show that bare keywords leave a default reading and that some models change typed text.
They also show that a supplied speech-act chip helps but is not always kept.
Families, requirements, harness, oracle tests, stored outputs and analysis code are available at \url{https://github.com/1unarzDev/aac_voice_preservation}.

\section*{Ethics and Positionality}
No data were collected from AAC users; AACText is public under CC BY 4.0.
All annotators were adult non-authors recruited from the authors' university classmates, identified only by an ID; each consented and was reimbursed for their time (payment and food).
They labelled model outputs and audited items under a fixed codebook, blinded to model, condition and source.
Some also chose keywords for public AACText messages, and some wrote new sentence pairs, which they released under CC~BY~4.0 with recorded consent.
No personal or health data were collected.
As independent researchers, we did not seek an institutional review determination.
No AAC users, speech-language pathologists or autistic advisers took part in this work, which limits it: every requirement here is our assumption about what matters, not a finding about what AAC users want, and the requirements should be revisited with AAC users before any deployment decision.
We drew on writing by and with autistic and AAC-using adults~\cite{zisk2019aac,donaldson2021everyone,frisch2026complicated} and on participatory-research and double-empathy work~\cite{nicolaidis2019aaspire,milton2012double} to avoid encoding a group style or deficit; that reading is not a substitute for participation.
We assume no \say{autistic style}, and our claims are limited to commitments explicit in the text.
