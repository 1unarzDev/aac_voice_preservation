\begin{abstract}
When a language model expands a few AAC keywords into a sentence, it can lose a \say{not}, and the person's words are then spoken as theirs.
Catching such failures needs a test oracle, an automated checker that is itself software and can be wrong, so we built a test harness and tested its oracle.
On human-written pairs, four models answered bare polarity keywords with an affirmative sentence in \pct{hwaff} of committed (valid affirmative or negated) outputs; supplying the missing cue, as a one-tap chip or a plain word, raised the intended speech act by \RES{hwp1}\,pp [\RES{t6p1_tlo}, \RES{t6p1_thi}] (\RES{wcp1}\,pp on a machine-written confirmation set), and the patterns replicate on \RES{mb_nmod} models from \RES{mb_nfam} families (plus a lite Gemini model on the legend arms).
On \RES{sc_nunits} elicited messages (three models, machine-judged), supplying the cue cuts expected polarity reversals from \RES{sc_pol_bare} to \RES{sc_pol_chip} per 1,000.
The oracle caught \kof{orr_j_mut} seeded one-value mutants of stored outputs with \kof{orr_j_fa} false alarms; its violation verdicts have \pct{nat_ho_viol_p} precision and \pct{nat_ho_viol_r} recall against human coders on two sets not used to build it; and trained people side with its tie-breaking arbiter in \pct{p19_arb} of \RES{p19_arb_n} codings.
Two defects in our own harness, each now a regression test, show the trade-off between false alarms and fault sensitivity in one family.
Passing the oracle is necessary but not sufficient: writers rejected \pct{wm_right_rej} of the oracle-passed outputs they rated, mostly for changed event or role structure.
Review by two AAC-user design consultants added a relations clause to R4, a new approval requirement (R6) and a lexical regression test (T1, not human-validated) that finds the statement chip realized as a spoken verb in \pct{t1} of statement-chip outputs.
The harness, tests and stored outputs are released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%% =====================================================================
Some people who use augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) compose a message from a few keywords, because each selection costs time and effort~\cite{valencia2023less,cai2024speakfaster}.
A language model then expands the keywords into a sentence~\cite{shen2022kwickchat,cai2024speakfaster}.
Typing \kw{supervisor read} might mean \say{The supervisor read} or \say{The supervisor did not read} (Fig.~\ref{fig:gallery}).
Whether the model erred depends on what the user supplied, and a text-similarity score cannot know that.
Symbol- and grid-based AAC is out of scope.

This paper asks which output changes count as failures, given exactly what the user supplied, and how to test the checker that decides.
The software-engineering problem is the oracle.
Deciding whether an output kept the meaning needs a checker, here an LLM that extracts a few explicit labels.
An unvalidated checker silently decides every result, so we treat testing the oracle as a first-class contribution beside testing the models.
We contribute:
(1)~a harness with six requirements R1--R6 that have stated sources and, where the oracle can see them, executable tests (\S\ref{sec:harness});
(2)~tests of the oracle, including a human check of its arbiter, a fault-by-test matrix, two defects we made ourselves, and the boundary of what the oracle checks (\S\ref{sec:oracle});
(3)~evidence on human-written pairs and on a machine-written confirmation set that the tests find real, model-specific behavior (\S\ref{sec:results});
(4)~a requirements review by two AAC-user design consultants, with each change traced (\S\ref{sec:consult}); and
(5)~the harness and stored outputs as an artifact.
The harness is for developers, not for assessing AAC users or their messages.

\section{Requirements and Harness}
\label{sec:harness}
%% =====================================================================
\paragraph*{Families and channels}
A \emph{family} is a minimal pair of source messages that share content words and differ on one \emph{critical} dimension: polarity, agent (who acts), certainty, speech act (question, request or statement), politeness, or time/quantity.
Each variant is rendered through five \emph{channels}, which say how much of the message the user supplied: \emph{bare} keywords; \emph{+word}, which adds one noncritical content word; \emph{+chip}, which adds the critical value as a chip such as \chip{NOT}; \emph{+all}, with every chip the variant needs; and \emph{full}, the source sentence itself.
Every variant needs at least one chip, so the unmarked reading gets a counter-chip (e.g., \chip{PLAIN}).
A chip is a \emph{probe} of whether supplied information is used, not a proposed interface: its real cost depends on the access method (a dwell, a menu traversal, a grid button), and it adds a vocabulary to learn and an error-recovery path.
The tested lever is the missing cue; the chip is one encoding of it, and two controls (Sec.~\ref{sec:results}) score an irrelevant chip and a plain word.

\paragraph*{Requirements}
For output $y$ from input $x$, let $L_d(y)$ be the extracted value on dimension $d$ and $C(x)$ the commitments that $x$ carries.
Table~\ref{tab:trace} states each requirement with its source and its test.
A \emph{Violation} means a supplied value was not kept (R1, R2); an \emph{Assumption} means the output committed to a value the input left open (R3).
A reference-fidelity score would charge the model for what the interface omitted, and a similarity score would pass an added \say{please} on a typed sentence.
R1--R5 are our proposal, derived from the structure of the task and from defects we met; two AAC-user design consultants then reviewed them and added R6 and refinements (\S\ref{sec:consult}).
The declared policy behind R2, keep typed wording unless the person asks for a rewrite and sees it first, protects authorship.
Some users prefer that a system rewrite their text, so R2 is a policy to test, not a recommendation.

\paragraph*{Harness}
Fig.~\ref{fig:harness} shows the pipeline.
A family specification is rendered into channels, and the R5 validator checks that each displayed chip matches the claim.
A generator produces outputs.
An extractor (gpt-6.1-sol; not a generator, but from the same family as the generator gpt-6.1-terra, so self-preference~\cite{zheng2023judging} is possible, and the human checks of Section~\ref{sec:oracle} are the mitigation) answers the family's critical question and five general questions from the output alone; added-content extraction also sees the visible input.
Two extractor prompts (J1, J2) differ in wording and option order, and critical disagreements go to a cross-family arbiter (a blind Claude Sonnet 5.5 session shown only the output text and the question; the same arbiter ruled on the human-written-pair, model-breadth and confirmation-set units) or stay unknown.
Verdicts are then computed in code from labels, item specification and channel, so a rule change re-scores every stored output without model calls.
The oracle decides only whether a fixed set of explicit, text-level values is kept.
It does not judge fluency, referents or whether anyone would accept the sentence.

\paragraph*{Cost of extending the harness}
Adding a \chip{THANKS} chip for politeness took \RES{ec_new_entry_lines} entry in the chip table (\texttt{src/labels.py}); the verdict code (\RES{ec_loc_attribute} lines) and the R5 validator (\RES{ec_loc_validator} lines) read that table and did not change, and re-scoring \RES{ec_rescored_rows} stored rows (all \RES{hw_ncand} candidate pairs, four models, \RES{ec_rows_per_pair_model} outputs per pair and model) took \RES{ec_rescore_seconds}\,s with \RES{ec_model_calls} model calls.
This is one dry run on one chip; a new dimension also needs a codebook option and extractor calls, whose cost we did not measure.

\paragraph*{Evidence sets}
All items are authored or crowd-written, not messages from AAC users.
The \emph{human-written pairs} (primary) are \RES{hw_nkept} pairs kept of \RES{hw_ncand} candidates, written by \RES{hw_nwriters} non-author writers over six dimensions and kept after three blind independent checks; \RES{hw_nfam} families are analysed, because \RES{hw_nexcl} speech-act family falls in the refusal/acceptance quarantine of Defect~A.
The \emph{confirmation set} has \RES{wc_n_fam} fresh machine-written families on three dimensions, whose three contrasts were pre-specified in a git-timestamped plan frozen before generation.
The \emph{development set} has \RES{n_fam_test} authored families used to develop prompts and codebooks.
\emph{Corpus transfer} uses crowd-written fictional messages from AACText~\cite{vertanen2011imagination}, and the \emph{composition study} (R4) adds a second chip to \RES{wd_nscen} scenarios.
Four models run with one fixed one-sentence prompt (greedy): Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Gemma-3-4B-it, three open models run locally with 4-bit quantization, and one API model, gpt-6.1-terra. Two larger open models, Qwen2.5-14B and Mistral-Nemo-12B, were added later under the same prompts (\RES{mb_nmod} models from \RES{mb_nfam} families on all arms), and a lite Gemini model ran the legend arms only (Section~\ref{sec:results}, Model breadth).
Unless labelled, intervals are pointwise 95\% percentile-bootstrap intervals from writer-cluster (human-written pairs) or seed/construction-cluster (confirmation set) resamples, 10,000 over a fixed model roster; the exceptions are the writer-level $t$ intervals of Table~\ref{tab:primary}, Wilson intervals for proportions in Table~\ref{tab:oracle}, and cluster-bootstrap $\kappa$ intervals.

\section{Results: Does the Harness Find Real Behavior?}
\label{sec:results}
%% =====================================================================
\subsection{Primary replication on human-written pairs}
Table~\ref{tab:primary} gives three contrasts.
\emph{Act-chip gain} is the speech-act chip minus a length-matched word.
\emph{Net politeness addition} is politeness added to plain typed sentences minus politeness removed from polite ones.
\emph{Affirmative default} is the share of committed (valid affirmative or negated) bare polarity outputs that are affirmative.
They were pre-specified for the confirmation set; on the human-written pairs they are an exploratory replication and were not preregistered.
Each rests on only \RES{hw_wr_min}--\RES{hw_wr_max} writers, so we recomputed inference at the writer level.
An exact sign-flip randomization over writers is in effect a sign test: with so few writers it gives the smallest possible $p$ ($\RES{t6p1_p1s}$, $\RES{t6p2_p1s}$, $\RES{t6p3_p1s}$; Holm \RES{t6p1_holm}, \RES{t6p2_holm}, \RES{t6p3_holm}) and cannot show magnitude.
What it shows is that every writer's mean is positive in all three contrasts, and the writer-level $t$ intervals of Table~\ref{tab:primary}, wider than the percentile-bootstrap ones, are the intervals to read; we claim direction and consistency, not width.
Effects vary by model for the act chip (Llama \RES{t6p1_llama}\,pp, gpt-6.1-terra \RES{t6p1_terra}) and for politeness (Gemma \RES{t6p2_gemma}, Llama \RES{t6p2_llama}).
The chip gain is positive on all six dimensions, between \RES{hw_gain_min} and \RES{hw_gain_max}\,pp, with every percentile-bootstrap lower bound above \RES{hw_gain_lo_min}\,pp.
No robustness check (near-duplicates collapsed, one writer excluded, arbiter calls reversed, all candidates) flips a decision (largest shift \RES{hw_shift_max}\,pp).
Because the cue text enters the model's prompt, Act-chip gain and Affirmative default are close to manipulation checks of whether the harness separates supplied from missing information, which is their purpose.
\paragraph*{Controls: the cue, not the chip}
Two controls on the human-written pairs (\texttt{analysis/chip\_controls/}) show that restoring the missing cue is what fixes the default.
An irrelevant chip, from another dimension and true of both sources, adds little to the critical value; the cue chip beats it by \RES{cc_irrel_min} to \RES{cc_irrel_max}\,pp by dimension, which rules out \say{any added chip helps}.
The same cue written as a plain word (\kw{not}, \kw{please}) is within \RES{cc_nl_near_max}\,pp of the chip on \RES{cc_nl_near} of \RES{cc_nl_dims} dimensions and beats it on speech act by \RES{cc_nl_act}\,pp.
The chip is the probe we used, not a requirement.
\paragraph*{Model breadth}
Qwen2.5-14B and Mistral-Nemo-12B (a fourth open family; same prompts, greedy, 4-bit) reproduce each pattern, so the default and the cue effect appear on \RES{mb_nmod} models from \RES{mb_nfam} families on all arms; a lite Gemini model, which ran only the legend arms, agrees. Per-model values are in the artifact.
\paragraph*{Same-items cue restoration and clinical weight}
On the same \RES{sc_nunits} elicited messages whose cue the typist dropped (three models, machine-judged), supplying the cue on every message cuts expected silent reversals per 1,000 messages from \RES{sc_pol_bare} to \RES{sc_pol_chip} (chip) and \RES{sc_pol_word} (word) for polarity, and from \RES{sc_pz_bare} to \RES{sc_pz_chip} and \RES{sc_pz_word} for politeness; a guard that regenerates when the chip check fails lifts the speech act kept from \RES{sc_act_chip}\% to \RES{sc_act_g1}\%.
Two speech-language pathologists (an expert review of stored outputs, $n=2$, not a population estimate) rated a dropped or added negation the most serious silent change (\RES{slp_neg} of 5 each, both would block it) and politeness the least; weighting failures by that severity leaves the ordering unchanged (guard \RES{slp_w_base_g}\% to \RES{slp_w_g1}\%, cue \RES{slp_w_base_hw}\% to \RES{slp_w_chip}\% with the chip; artifact).

\subsection{Confirmation set}
All three contrasts have the same sign on the confirmation set, and every interval excludes zero (Table~\ref{tab:primary}).
Act-chip gain (\RES{wcp1}\,pp) is a little below the human-written \RES{hwp1}\,pp, and the Affirmative default is of similar size.
Net politeness addition is small, with a lower bound of \RES{wcp2_lo}\,pp.
Confirmation is qualified: \RES{wc_prefreeze} cached requests predate the final item freeze, and Act-chip gain relies on post-hoc arbitration of extractor disagreements (\RES{wc_arb_rows} rows); with those labels masked as unknown it is \RES{wcp1_um}\,pp (last row of Table~\ref{tab:primary}).
Net politeness addition and Affirmative default contain no arbitrated rows.
\say{Pre-specified and git-timestamped} means the plan was committed to a private repository before generation, which is not independently verifiable; a hash of the protocol was deposited publicly as GitHub release \texttt{\RES{hash_release}}, after the confirmation-set freeze, so it does not certify it.

\subsection{Assumptions, full-input violations and model heterogeneity}
Table~\ref{tab:ladder} separates the two kinds of evidence.
In the italic cells the input is ambiguous, so the match is the model's default, bounded by a 50\% design ceiling; it is not accuracy.
In the other cells the chip or sentence is visible, so the match measures preservation.
Under bare input, \kn{wcaff} committed directional polarity outputs on the confirmation set were affirmative; these are Assumptions, not failures, and the interface decides who pays an extra selection to say \say{not}.
To test whether the default is a compression artifact we asked humans: \RES{el_nann} annotators typed keywords for \RES{el_nmsg} crowd-written AACText messages (\RES{el_nsets} sets).
On the \RES{el_neg_c_n} messages with a negation cue they dropped it in \pct{el_neg_h} of sets, against \pct{el_neg_c} for a crude four-word compressor.
The affirmative default is also the better guess: among human keyword strings with no negation word, only \pct{t2_post} \cip{t2_post} came from a negated message, and the models answered affirmatively for \pct{t2_modelshare} of them.
Its errors are silent: about \RES{t2_rev} negated messages per 1,000 are reversed without any signal, against \RES{t2_spur} spurious negations, so the harness needs R3 to log the Assumption and test its direction.
A pre-declared three-arm test shows the default is not a prompt-legend artifact: the affirmative share among committed bare polarity outputs was \pct{lg_a} with the legend, \pct{lg_b} with none and \pct{lg_c} when the prompt also said markers may be missing, across \RES{lg_nmodels} models and both sets.
The affirmative default is not new: Chen et al.~\cite{chen2023say} reported keyword-to-sentence models writing wrongly positive sentences about negative commonsense facts. We price it for AAC input against a human base rate, show that it survives prompting and holds on \RES{mb_nmod} models, and separate it from cue violations and compression loss.
Models almost never answered \say{unclear} (at most \pct{lg_unclear_max}), so prompting does not replace the test.

\subsection{Composition (R4)}
\label{sec:comp}
The composition study adds a second chip to a single-chip input and counts pass$\to$fail transitions of the first chip's value against the disagreement between two greedy repeats (the floor).
It has \RES{wd_nscen} scenarios, nine inputs each, two encodings of the speech-act chip (\chip{TELL} and its alias \chip{STATEMENT}), four models and two draws (\RES{wd_nreq} requests).
Failures depend on both model and encoding: Qwen loses the agent in \kn{wdqa} transitions under \chip{TELL} (\RES{wdqa_d}\,pp \ci{wdqa_d}) and in \RES{wdqa_stmt_k} under \chip{STATEMENT}; Llama loses polarity in \kn{wdll} transitions under \chip{STATEMENT} (\RES{wdll_d}\,pp \ci{wdll_d}) and in \RES{wd_tellpol_llama_k} under \chip{TELL}.
Two greedy repeats of the identical single-chip input disagreed on \kn{wdqa_rep} of Qwen and \kn{wdll_rep} of Llama inputs (\texttt{analysis/r2d/}), so the raw rate is the floor-adjusted rate; on two-chip inputs the stricter floor leaves \RES{wdqa_db} and \RES{wdll_db}\,pp.
An R4 test must therefore fix the encoding: a failure under one encoding of the same meaning is an encoding defect before it is a model property.
The study is exploratory and machine-scored.

\section{Testing the Oracle}
\label{sec:oracle}
%% =====================================================================
Every result above is only as good as the checker, so we test it as software, in the spirit of the test-oracle literature~\cite{weyuker1982nontestable,barr2015oracle} and of mutation analysis~\cite{jia2011mutation}: an authorized change must pass and an unauthorized one must fail.
\emph{Identity test:} an unchanged source injected as its own full-channel output must pass.
\emph{Scoped mutants:} a hand-written one-value change to a development-set source must be detected.
\emph{Keyed challenge:} hand-written controls and one-feature mutants, with keys hashed before the first extractor call, must be classified as keyed.
\emph{Human gate:} extractor labels against human coders.

\paragraph*{Two defects, two regression tests}
Two defects arose while building the harness; neither was seeded, and each now has a regression test (summarized in the fault-by-test matrix below).
In Defect A, the identity test failed \RES{id_fail_raw} of \RES{id_n} reproductions, all one sentence, \say{Yes, you can borrow my car this weekend.}, coded \emph{\RES{id_fail_got}} against the target \emph{\RES{id_fail_want}}: the model had done its job and the checker demanded a distinction the text need not force.
The obvious repair, accepting \emph{statement} for an acceptance target, passed identity but failed the next test: the relaxed oracle detected only \kof{or_acc_mut_det} acceptance mutants and missed both statement substitutions (\say{You borrowed my car this weekend.}), whereas without the relaxation it detected \kof{or_new_mut_det_raw} mutants across groups.
This is one sentence in one family, a demonstration of the trade-off and not an estimate of how often relaxation hides faults: removing a false alarm removed fault sensitivity, so both refusal/acceptance families are quarantined from every primary result (leaving \RES{n_fam_primary} of \RES{n_fam_test} development families) until a predicate passes both tests; keyed acceptance mutants now guard the rule.
In Defect B, an audit of \RES{wb_msgs_restored} restored-chip inputs built from AACText messages found \RES{wb_conflicts} whose displayed chip contradicted the stored source, e.g., \chip{MAYBE} for a plain assertion, after a silent fallback in our builder; in the \RES{wb_maybe_msgs} \chip{MAYBE} messages, all \RES{wb_maybe_n} outputs followed the visible chip and failed the source, which a source-fidelity score counts as model failures.
The R5 validator decodes only the rendered input and now rejects all \RES{r5_conf} before generation.
Replayed on every chip input of the confirmation set, the composition study and the human-written pairs (\RES{t9_inputs} inputs), it found \RES{t9_conflicts} conflicts; \RES{t9_compat} were verified compatible and \RES{t9_unknown} (time chips and one long chip alias) are outside its decoder.
It covers chip inputs only, matches the audit on \RES{r5_agree}/\RES{r5_n}, and never certifies the \RES{r5_sure} coarser \chip{SURE} restorations.

\paragraph*{Mutation testing on real outputs, with baselines}
Authored sentences are easy, so we also seeded one-value mutants into real stored outputs of the human-written pairs (seed fixed in advance; \RES{orr_nops} operators re-implemented because the authored mutants were hand-written) and drew \RES{orr_nctl} surface-edited controls (case, punctuation, contraction; \RES{orr_nboost} boosters, which the contract tolerates, are excluded from the mutants) from outputs the oracle scored as preserving.
The \RES{orr_j_mut_den} mutants come from \RES{orr_nsrc} distinct stored sentences, so the intervals treat them as more independent than they are.
Table~\ref{tab:oracle} gives the result: the judge caught \kof{orr_j_mut} mutants with \kof{orr_j_fa} false alarms, matching the authored-sentence result (\kof{orr_wa}).
Its \RES{orr_nmiss} misses are two mixed-signal time shifts and one equivalent mutant.
A surface lexicon (negation, hedge, politeness, tense and question cues) scores \kof{orr_s_mut} with \kof{orr_s_fa} false alarms, and string overlap \kof{orr_o_mut} with \kof{orr_o_fa}.
The lexicon is near-circular, since it matches the cues the operators insert and delete; the judge's edge shows mainly on controls.

\paragraph*{The oracle on faults nobody seeded}
Human-labelled sets of natural model outputs test the oracle on faults nobody seeded.
We took the verdict-level counts that the gold-set scoring already computes (judge verdict against human-consensus verdict), with violation and preservation failure as the positive classes.
The \emph{development items} (\RES{nat_dev_nunits_viol} verdicts) built the codebook and extractor prompts, so they are reported separately; two \emph{held-out sets}, a second gold set of fresh outputs and the human-written pairs (\RES{nat_ho_nunits_viol} verdicts), were not used to build either.
On the held-out sets, violation verdicts have precision \pct{nat_ho_viol_p} \cip{nat_ho_viol_p} and recall \pct{nat_ho_viol_r} \cip{nat_ho_viol_r} (\RES{nat_ho_viol_npos} positives), and preservation failures \pct{nat_ho_pres_p} \cip{nat_ho_pres_p} and \pct{nat_ho_pres_r} \cip{nat_ho_pres_r} (Table~\ref{tab:oracle}).
The development items give \pct{nat_dev_viol_p} and \pct{nat_dev_viol_r} for violations, and pooling all three sets, which includes the items that built the codebook and so is in-sample, gives \pct{nat_viol_p} and \pct{nat_viol_r} (\RES{nat_viol_npos} positives).
On the elicited keywords the assumption verdict has precision \RES{nat_elic_p}\% and recall \RES{nat_elic_r}\% on \RES{nat_elic_n} items.
Each set has only \RES{nat_viol_posmin}--\RES{nat_viol_posmax} violation positives, hence the wide intervals.

\paragraph*{Human gate}
The first human gate, with two annotators, was run on the development items (the extraction inventory used to develop the codebook), with plurality references and tied items excluded rather than adjudicated.
Its pre-specified criterion ($\kappa\geq0.6$) passes on all \RES{human_gate_n} fields.
It validates extraction of explicit markers on those items, not repaired text or corpus-transfer readings.
Two later checks use outputs the harness had not been developed on: outputs for human-elicited keywords and outputs for human-written pairs (Table~\ref{tab:oracle}).
Recomputed with the stricter rule that ties and unsure count as disagreement, critical $\kappa$ on the test split is \RES{t7g_k} (development items), \RES{t7e_k} \ci{t7e} (human-elicited keywords) and \RES{t7h_k} \ci{t7h} (human-written pairs), so the gate still passes; the weakest field is added content on human-written pairs (\RES{t7h_ac_k}, lower bound \RES{t7h_ac_lo}).
Those checks are the oracle's evidence on the headline-type data, though their coders were also taught the codebook, and the arbiter belongs to a family that no generator or extractor does, so people siding with it is also our main evidence against self-preference.
A third check targets the arbiter, whose rulings settle every critical disagreement between J1 and J2.
Of \RES{p19_arb_n} codings by \RES{p19_nann} non-author annotators of units the arbiter decided, people sided with the arbiter in \RES{p19_arb_k}/\RES{p19_arb_n} codings (\pct{p19_arb}, $\kappa=\RES{p19_kappa}$), against \pct{p19_j1} for J1 and \pct{p19_j2} for J2 ($\kappa=\RES{p19_j2k}$).
People sided with the arbiter, not with either prompt alone.
Machine agreement is not validation: on \say{I can't wait to go to the party.}, both extractor prompts, the rule-based extractor and two further machine coders answered \emph{negated}, and both human annotators answered \emph{affirmative}, as keyed.

\paragraph*{Necessary, not sufficient}
The oracle checks a fixed set of explicit values, so passing it is necessary for a faithful sentence, not sufficient.
In the writer-intent check, \RES{p18_nwriters} writers judged \RES{p18_acc_n} outputs for their own pairs: the cue raised the share called the intended version by \RES{p18_gain}\,pp [\RES{p18_gain_lo}, \RES{p18_gain_hi}], yet writers accepted \RES{p18_acc_k} of \RES{p18_acc_n} as written, and writer--judge $\kappa$ is \RES{p18_kappa}.
Of the \RES{wm_right_n} outputs whose critical value the oracle scored correct, \RES{wm_right_rej_den} had an intended-version rating, and writers said \RES{wm_right_rej_k} of those (\pct{wm_right_rej}) were not the version they meant.
The reasons are not added content (the primary reason in \RES{wm_added_prim_k} of \RES{wm_notacc_n} rejections) but compression loss and misresolved events: \pct{wm_dropped} of the rejected, oracle-correct outputs lack words from the writer's own sentence that no keyword or chip supplies, and \pct{wm_subst} show a different event or role.
Of \RES{slp_jp_n} SLP rater-judgments on oracle-passed outputs, \RES{slp_jp_unacc} said the sentence only partly or did not say what the selected words mean, and both consultants asked that relations (who does what) be preserved, which R4 now says.
Event and role structure is a missing requirement, not a fault of the oracle, which answers whether a supplied value was kept; a separate test must ask whether the sentence says the same thing.
The cheapest such check, T1, requires that a \chip{TELL} input whose keywords hold no form of \kw{tell} not yield \kw{tell/tells/told/telling}.
On the human-written pairs \RES{t1_k} of \RES{t1_n} such outputs (\pct{t1}; \RES{t1_fam_k} of \RES{t1_fam_n} families) do, against \pct{t1_ctrl} of same-source bare outputs; Qwen does in \pct{t1_qwen}, gpt-6.1-terra in \pct{t1_terra}.
T1 is lexical and not human-validated; the critical check passes these outputs because the speech act is a statement.

Two paid AAC-user design consultants reviewed R1--R5 in plain language with real stored examples and a description of the chip and plain-word designs.

The relations change led to a test: an example the oracle had passed, a \chip{TELL} turned into a verb, was flagged as unsendable, so we wrote T1 and measured it on all stored outputs (\S\ref{sec:oracle}).

The consultants are not a sample of AAC users, and the requirements stay our proposals.

\section{Related Work}
%% =====================================================================
\paragraph*{Test oracles, metamorphic testing and requirements for ML}
Whether an output is correct is the oracle problem~\cite{barr2015oracle}; partial or pseudo oracles are standard~\cite{weyuker1982nontestable}, and metamorphic testing checks relations between runs~\cite{chen2018metamorphic}.
Mutation analysis measures a test suite by whether it detects seeded faults~\cite{jia2011mutation}; we apply it to the oracle, not the system.
Requirements engineering for ML calls for explicit, testable requirements~\cite{vogelsang2019re4ml}; R1--R6 are one such set, with sources and tests stated.

\paragraph*{Testing LLM systems}
CheckList organizes behavioral tests into minimum-functionality, invariance and directional tests~\cite{ribeiro2020checklist}, and Cho et al.\ catalogue metamorphic relations for LLM-based NLP~\cite{cho2025metamorphic}; we claim no new testing technique.
LLM judges show position, verbosity and self-preference biases~\cite{zheng2023judging}, and Pauli et al.\ show that preservation metrics look valid when test data confound style change with content errors~\cite{pauli2025stylegap}; our identity and mutant tests apply that idea to a checker.

\paragraph*{Preservation and LLMs in AAC}
Qu et al.\ evaluate speech-act retention in style transfer~\cite{qu2023conditioning}, and rewriting can keep semantic similarity while erasing pragmatic markers~\cite{navneet2026voice}.
AAC systems studied with users expose user-side controls over context or stance~\cite{valencia2023less,valencia2024compa}; a Lacosse et al.\ preprint keeps a short list of bracketed function words, including \say{not}~\cite{lacosse2026cognitivesteering}; Choudhury et al.\ test whether affect cues reach the output~\cite{choudhury2025alignment}; R-SPEAK was co-designed with people with aphasia and compared LLMs on interpreting aphasic speech~\cite{altamimi2026rspeak}.
We add a conditional specification of which commitments are testable under each input, and tests of the oracle that checks them.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}
%% =====================================================================
\emph{Construct.} Labels are coarse, explicit categories; \say{plain} means no explicit marker, and referents, negation scope, certainty degree and event structure are only partly covered (\S\ref{sec:oracle}).
Passing the checker is weaker than keeping the typed wording, and a scored repair is not necessarily a sentence someone could say.
\emph{Oracle.} All rates depend on an LLM extractor and arbiter, and the extractor shares a family with one generator; the arbiter ran after outcomes were known, the arbiter check covers its rulings, not every label, and human coders were taught the codebook.
The defect trace has two defects, both found by our own process; it shows that the process works, not that it finds defects we did not anticipate.
The surface baseline is near-circular.
\emph{Internal.} The confirmation set has early cached requests and post-hoc arbitration; the human-written pairs are not preregistered and have few writers per contrast.
\emph{Use.} No outside developer has used the harness yet. The SLP panel is two raters and the consultants two people; both shaped the requirements, neither establishes what AAC users in general want, and writer-intent agreement is not validation by AAC users.
\emph{External.} \RES{mb_nmod} models from \RES{mb_nfam} families (open models at most 14B and 4-bit quantized, no frontier-size open model), plus one lite Gemini model on the legend arms only; one prompt; English; constructed and crowd-written, not AAC-user, messages; API models may be retired and are not bit-reproducible.
\emph{Conclusion.} Effects are model-specific, and zero observed events do not establish zero risk.
Selection counts omit reading, navigation and fatigue (see also the position paper~\cite{frisch2026complicated}).

\section{Conclusion}
%% =====================================================================
An oracle for meaning preservation is software and needs tests of its own.
Ours caught a repair that hid real substitutions and a builder that supplied the wrong chip, its labels agree with trained human coders on two sets of outputs, and people side with its arbiter on \pct{p19_arb} of disputed labels.
With it, human-written pairs show that bare keywords leave a default reading, that supplying the missing cue, as a chip or a plain word, removes most expected reversals, and that some models change typed text.
The tests also mark their own edge: passing the oracle is necessary but not sufficient, because event and role structure is a requirement it does not check, and review by two AAC-user consultants added relations to R4, a new approval requirement (R6) and a first regression test for the missing structure.
Families, requirements, harness, oracle tests, stored outputs and analysis code are available at \url{https://github.com/1unarzDev/aac_voice_preservation}.

\section*{Ethics and Positionality}
No data about AAC users were collected; AACText is public under CC BY 4.0.
All annotators were adult non-authors, classmates of the authors from a university (we list no affiliation because the work was not run through an institution), identified only by an ID; each consented and was reimbursed for their time (payment and food).
They labelled model outputs and audited items under a fixed codebook, blinded to model, condition and source.
Some also chose keywords for public AACText messages, and some wrote new sentence pairs, which they released under CC~BY~4.0 with recorded consent.
No personal or health data were collected.
As independent researchers, we did not seek an institutional review determination.
Two speech-language pathologists were paid, anonymous expert annotators of study materials under a written professional-services agreement; the data are about the outputs, not about them.
Two AAC-user consultants were paid design consultants who reviewed requirements and examples under a written consulting agreement; we did not collect, analyse or report their experiences, and neither package collected demographic or health information.
The requirements were reviewed by the consultants and the outputs rated by the SLPs as described above; they are still our proposals, and we make no claim about benefit to AAC users.
We drew on writing by and with autistic and AAC-using adults~\cite{zisk2019aac,donaldson2021everyone,frisch2026complicated} and on participatory-research and double-empathy work~\cite{nicolaidis2019aaspire,milton2012double} to avoid encoding a group style or deficit.
We assume no \say{autistic style}, and our claims are limited to commitments explicit in the text.
