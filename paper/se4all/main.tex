\begin{abstract}
Developers of LLM keyword-to-sentence expansion for augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) need tests for whether a person's meaning survives, but the test oracle, an automated checker of what the output says, is itself software that can be wrong.
We present a small evaluation harness built around that problem: five input-conditioned requirements that score a choice only when the input carried it, minimal-pair families rendered through five input channels, an LLM-extractor oracle whose verdicts are computed in code, and tests of the oracle itself.
On \RES{hw_nkept} human-written pairs from \RES{hw_nwriters} writers (an exploratory replication, not preregistered, with \RES{hw_wr_min}--\RES{hw_wr_max} writers per contrast), four models gave the affirmative reading on \pct{hwaff} of bare polarity outputs, a speech-act tag raised preservation by \RES{hwp1}\,pp over a length-matched word \ci{hwp1}, and fully typed plain requests gained a politeness marker in \pct{hwpzadd} of outputs against \pct{hwpzrem} removals, driven mainly by one model.
The direction of all three results held on a machine-written confirmatory set whose contrasts were pre-specified and git-timestamped.
Testing the oracle mattered: relaxing a rule to remove a false alarm hid real substitutions, and an input-builder defect (\RES{wb_conflicts}/\RES{wb_msgs_restored} restorations) was found by auditing against an executable requirement, which now rejects it before generation.
The oracle's critical labels agreed with human coders at $\kappa=\RES{jve_kappa}$ and $\RES{jvh_kappa}$ on two new sets of outputs.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%% =====================================================================
Many people who use AAC with letter or word selection compose a message from a few keywords, because each selection costs time and effort~\cite{valencia2023less,cai2024speakfaster}; LLM systems expand such a selection into a sentence~\cite{shen2022kwickchat,cai2024speakfaster}.
Symbol- and grid-based AAC is out of scope here.
A test for such a system must decide whether an edit is a defect, and that depends on what the input carried.
From the keywords \kw{supervisor read} no model can know whether the supervisor does \emph{not} read; the affirmative guess is a default, not a violation (Fig.~\ref{fig:gallery}, card~1).
When a person supplies a cue, or a whole sentence, the output must keep it, and a changed value is a violation.

The software-engineering problem is the oracle.
Deciding whether an output ``kept the meaning'' needs a checker, here an LLM that extracts a few explicit labels, and an unvalidated checker silently decides every result.
We therefore treat \emph{testing the oracle} as a first-class contribution beside testing the models.
We contribute:
(1)~a harness with five requirements R1--R5 that have stated sources and executable tests (\S\ref{sec:harness});
(2)~tests of the oracle, including two defects we introduced ourselves, each traced from symptom to regression test (\S\ref{sec:oracle});
(3)~evidence from a human-written replication (hw1, primary) and a machine-written confirmatory set (W-C) that these tests find real, model-specific behavior (\S\ref{sec:results}); and
(4)~the harness and stored outputs as an artifact.
The harness is for developers; it is not a tool for assessing AAC users or their messages.
We ran no study with AAC users and make no claim about benefit, effort or preference.

\section{Requirements and Harness}
\label{sec:harness}
%% =====================================================================
\paragraph*{Families and channels}
A \emph{family} is a minimal pair of source messages that share content words and differ on one \emph{critical} dimension: polarity, agent (who acts), certainty, speech act (question, request or statement), politeness, or time/quantity.
Each variant is rendered through five channels: \emph{bare} keywords; \emph{+word}, which adds one noncritical content word; \emph{+chip}, which adds the critical cue as a bracketed tag (\chip{NOT}); \emph{+all}, with every tag the variant needs; and \emph{full}, the source sentence itself.
Every variant needs at least one tag, so the unmarked reading gets a counter-tag (e.g., \chip{PLAIN}).
A chip is a \emph{probe} of whether supplied information is used, not a proposed interface: its real cost depends on the access method (a dwell, a menu traversal, a grid button), and it adds a vocabulary to learn and an error-recovery path.

\paragraph*{Requirements}
For output $y$ from input $x$, let $L_d(y)$ be the extracted value on dimension $d$ and $C(x)$ the commitments that $x$ carries.
Table~\ref{tab:trace} states each requirement with its source and its test.
A \emph{Violation} means a supplied value was not kept (R1, R2); an \emph{Assumption} means the output committed to a value the input left open (R3).
A reference-fidelity score would instead charge the model for what the interface omitted, and a similarity score would pass an added \say{please} on a typed sentence.
All five requirements are our proposal, derived from the structure of the task and from defects we met; none was derived with AAC users or clinicians.
The declared policy behind R2, do not change typed wording unless the person asks for rewriting, protects authorship; some users prefer that a system rewrite their text, so R2 is a policy to test, not a recommendation.

\paragraph*{Harness}
Fig.~\ref{fig:harness} shows the pipeline.
A family specification is rendered into channels; the R5 validator checks that each displayed cue matches the claim; a generator produces outputs; an extractor (gpt-6.1-sol, a different model from the one under test) answers the family's critical question and five general questions from the output alone, with added-content extraction also seeing the visible input.
Two extractor prompts (J1, J2) differ in wording and option order; critical disagreements go to a cross-family arbiter (claude-opus-5-5) or stay unknown.
Verdicts are then computed in code from labels, item specification and channel, so a rule change re-scores every stored output without model calls.
The oracle decides only whether a fixed set of explicit, text-level values is kept; it does not judge fluency, referents or whether anyone would accept the sentence.

\paragraph*{Reuse}
A developer adds a requirement as a function of labels, item specification and channel, re-scores stored outputs without any model call, and runs the identity and keyed-mutant tests before trusting the new rule.
Re-scoring and the oracle tests need no API access; regenerating outputs needs the API models, whose versions may be retired and whose outputs are not bit-reproducible.

\paragraph*{Evidence populations}
All items are authored or crowd-written, not messages from AAC users.
\emph{hw1} (primary): \RES{hw_nkept} pairs kept of \RES{hw_ncand} candidates, written by \RES{hw_nwriters} non-author writers over six dimensions, each kept after three blind independent checks; \RES{hw_nfam} families are analysed.
\emph{W-C} (confirmatory, machine-written): \RES{wc_n_fam} fresh families on three dimensions, whose three contrasts were pre-specified in a git-timestamped plan frozen before generation.
\emph{Discovery}: \RES{n_fam_test} authored families, used to develop prompts and codebooks.
\emph{W-B}: crowd-written fictional messages from AACText~\cite{vertanen2011imagination}, used for corpus transfer (\S\ref{sec:oracle}).
Four models run with a single one-sentence prompt (P0, greedy): Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Gemma-3-4B-it, three open models run locally with 4-bit quantization, and one API model, gpt-6.1-terra.
All intervals are pointwise 95\% percentile intervals from writer-cluster (hw1) or seed/construction-cluster (W-C) bootstraps, with 10,000 resamples over a fixed model roster.

\subsection{Primary replication on human-written pairs (hw1)}
Table~\ref{tab:primary} gives the three contrasts, which we name \emph{Act-gain} (speech-act chip minus a length-matched word), \emph{Politeness} (politeness added to plain typed sentences minus removed from polite ones) and \emph{Default} (share of bare polarity outputs that are affirmative).
They were pre-specified for W-C as P1--P3 (unrelated to prompts); in hw1 they are a replication and were not preregistered.
All hold under Holm correction, but each rests on only \RES{hw_wr_min}--\RES{hw_wr_max} writers, so we claim direction and consistency, not interval width; a writer-cluster percentile interval with so few clusters is probably too narrow.
The chip gain is positive on all six dimensions, between \RES{hw_gain_min} and \RES{hw_gain_max}\,pp, with every lower bound above \RES{hw_gain_lo_min}\,pp.
Collapsing near-duplicate pairs, excluding one writer, reversing the arbiter's judgment calls or using all candidates flips no decision; the largest shift is \RES{hw_shift_max}\,pp (\RES{hw_shift_all}\,pp with all candidates).
Because the chip text enters the model's prompt, Act-gain and Default are close to manipulation checks of whether the harness separates supplied from missing information, which is their purpose.

\subsection{Confirmatory machine-written set (W-C)}
The same three contrasts hold on W-C with the same direction and a similar or smaller size (Table~\ref{tab:primary}).
Confirmation is qualified.
First, \RES{wc_prefreeze} cached requests predate the final item freeze.
Second, Act-gain relies on post-hoc arbitration of judge disagreements (\RES{wc_arb_rows} rows); with those labels masked as unknown it is \RES{wcp1_um}\,pp \ci{wcp1_um}, a sensitivity analysis, and Politeness and Default contain no arbitrated rows.
``Pre-specified and git-timestamped'' means the plan was committed to a private repository before generation; it is not independently verifiable.
A hash of the protocol was deposited publicly as GitHub release \texttt{\RES{hash_release}}; it post-dates the W-C freeze and so does not certify it.

Fig.~\ref{fig:ladder} separates the two kinds of evidence.
Under bare input, \kn{wcaff} directional polarity outputs in W-C were affirmative; these are Assumptions, not failures, and the interface decides who pays an extra selection to say \say{not}.
That default may be an artifact of heavy compression, so we asked humans: \RES{el_nann} annotators typed keywords for \RES{el_nmsg} crowd-written AACText messages (\RES{el_nsets} sets); on the \RES{el_neg_c_n} messages with a negation cue they dropped it in \pct{el_neg_h} of sets, against \pct{el_neg_c} for a deliberately crude four-word rule-based compressor, so the Assumption channel is realistic but the compressor is not a user model.
Full-input violations are model-specific: in hw1 the Politeness contrast is \RES{hwpz_gemma}\% of outputs for Gemma, \RES{hwpz_terra}\% for gpt-6.1-terra, \RES{hwpz_llama}\% for Llama and \RES{hwpz_qwen}\% for Qwen (net additions minus removals); in W-C, plain typed sentences gained a politeness marker in \kn{wcpzadd} outputs, driven by Gemma (\kn{wcpzadd_gemma}) and gpt-6.1-terra (\kn{wcpzadd_terra}), while Llama and Qwen removed markers more often than they added them.
This is a regression to test for per model, not a property of LLMs (Fig.~\ref{fig:ratchet}).
Question tags are the weakest supplied cue: one tag kept the act in \pct{wcm_act_chip1} of W-C targets, and \RES{wc_q2req_k} of the \RES{wc_qfail_k} failures were coded as requests (\say{Can you\ldots?}), a codebook decision that we flag rather than resolve.

\section{Testing the Oracle}
\label{sec:oracle}
%% =====================================================================
Every result above is only as good as the checker, so we test it as software, in the spirit of the test-oracle literature~\cite{weyuker1982nontestable,barr2015oracle} and of mutation analysis~\cite{jia2011mutation}: an authorized change must pass and an unauthorized one must fail.
\emph{Identity test:} an unchanged source injected as its own full-channel output must pass.
\emph{Keyed mutants:} a hand-written one-value change, with keys hashed before the first judge call, must be detected.
\emph{Human gate:} extractor labels against human coders.

\paragraph*{Two defects, two regression tests}
Table~\ref{tab:trace2} traces the two defects we introduced ourselves.
In Defect A, the identity test failed \RES{id_fail_raw} of \RES{id_n} reproductions, all one sentence, \say{Yes, you can borrow my car this weekend.}, coded \emph{\RES{id_fail_got}} against the target \emph{\RES{id_fail_want}}.
The model had done its job; the checker demanded a distinction the text need not force.
The obvious repair, accepting \emph{statement} for an acceptance target, passed identity but failed the next test: the relaxed oracle detected only \kof{or_acc_mut_det} acceptance mutants and missed both statement substitutions (\say{You borrowed my car this weekend.}), whereas without the relaxation it detected \kof{or_new_mut_det_raw} mutants across groups.
Removing a false alarm removed fault sensitivity, so both refusal/acceptance families are quarantined from every primary result (leaving \RES{n_fam_primary} of \RES{n_fam_test} discovery families) until a predicate passes both tests.
In Defect B, an audit of \RES{wb_msgs_restored} restored-cue inputs built from AACText messages found \RES{wb_conflicts} whose displayed chip contradicted the stored source, e.g., \chip{MAYBE} for a plain assertion, after a silent fallback in our builder.
In the \RES{wb_maybe_msgs} \chip{MAYBE} messages, all \RES{wb_maybe_n} outputs followed the visible chip and failed the source, which a source-fidelity score counts as model failures.
The R5 validator, which decodes only the rendered input, now rejects all \RES{r5_conf} before generation, matches the audit on \RES{r5_agree}/\RES{r5_n}, and never certifies the \RES{r5_sure} coarser \chip{SURE} restorations.

\paragraph*{Human gate}
The first human gate, with two annotators, was run on the \emph{discovery} items (the extraction inventory used to develop the codebook), with plurality references and tied items excluded rather than adjudicated; its pre-specified criterion ($\kappa\geq0.6$) passes on all \RES{human_gate_n} fields.
It validates extraction of explicit markers on those items, not repaired text or W-B readings.
Two later checks use outputs the harness had not been developed on: outputs for human-elicited keywords and outputs for hw1 pairs (Table~\ref{tab:oracle}).
They are the oracle's evidence on the headline-type data, though their coders were also taught the codebook, and the verdict $\kappa$ values on hw1 rest on small counts of positive verdicts.
Machine agreement is not validation: on \say{I can't wait to go to the party.}, both judge prompts, the rule-based extractor and two further machine coders answered \emph{negated}, and both human annotators answered \emph{affirmative}, as keyed.

\section{Related Work}
%% =====================================================================
\paragraph*{Test oracles, metamorphic testing and requirements for ML}
Whether an output is correct is the oracle problem~\cite{barr2015oracle}; for programs without a practical oracle, partial or pseudo oracles are standard~\cite{weyuker1982nontestable}, and metamorphic testing checks relations between runs instead~\cite{chen2018metamorphic}.
Mutation analysis measures a test suite by whether it detects seeded faults~\cite{jia2011mutation}; we apply that idea to the oracle, not the system.
Requirements engineering for ML stresses that data-dependent behavior needs explicit, testable requirements~\cite{vogelsang2019re4ml}; our R1--R5 are one such set, with sources and tests stated.

\paragraph*{Testing LLM systems}
CheckList organizes behavioral tests into minimum-functionality, invariance and directional tests~\cite{ribeiro2020checklist}; Cho et al.\ catalogue metamorphic relations for LLM-based NLP~\cite{cho2025metamorphic}; COLLIE tests compositional constraints~\cite{yao2024collie}; Sensei combines a user simulator with assertions and metamorphic relations~\cite{delara2026sensei}.
We claim no new testing technique.
LLM judges show position, verbosity and self-preference biases~\cite{zheng2023judging,panickssery2024selfpreference}, and similarity metrics can miss negation~\cite{koch2022selfeval}.
Pauli et al.\ show that preservation metrics look valid when test data confound style change with content errors~\cite{pauli2025stylegap}; our identity and keyed-mutant tests apply that idea to a checker.

\paragraph*{Preservation and LLMs in AAC}
Qu et al.\ evaluate speech-act retention in style transfer~\cite{qu2023conditioning}; rewriting can keep semantic similarity while erasing pragmatic markers~\cite{navneet2026voice}; and LLMs favor particular politeness strategies~\cite{zhao2025politeness}.
AAC systems studied with users expose intent controls~\cite{valencia2023less,valencia2024compa,mao2025designprobes,xiang2025socializechat}; Lacosse et al.\ keep bracketed function words such as \say{not}~\cite{lacosse2026cognitivesteering}; Choudhury et al.\ test whether affect cues reach the output~\cite{choudhury2025alignment}; Gorenshtein et al.\ benchmark intent drift~\cite{gorenshtein2026intentdrift}; R-SPEAK scores faithfulness and was co-designed with people with aphasia~\cite{altamimi2026rspeak}.
We add a conditional specification of which commitments are testable under each input, and tests of the oracle that checks them.
Accessibility engineering spans the life cycle~\cite{paiva2021accessibility}, but conformance checks do not see whether generated text keeps a user's meaning.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}
%% =====================================================================
\emph{Construct.} Labels are coarse, explicit categories; \say{plain} means no explicit marker, and referents, negation scope and certainty degree are only partly covered.
Passing the checker is weaker than keeping the typed wording, and a scored repair is not necessarily a sentence someone could say; a small human audit of one companion repair method, not reported here, supports that caution.
\emph{Oracle.} All rates depend on an LLM extractor and arbiter; the arbiter ran after outcomes were known, and human coders were taught the codebook.
The first human gate's $\kappa$ is on discovery items, not on confirmatory data.
\emph{Internal.} W-C has early cached requests and post-hoc arbitration; hw1 is not preregistered and has few writers per contrast.
\emph{External.} Four models, three small and quantized; one prompt; English; constructed and crowd-written, not AAC-user, messages.
Model versions may be retired, and API outputs are not bit-reproducible.
\emph{Conclusion.} Effects are model-specific, and zero observed events do not establish zero risk.
Selection counts omit reading, navigation and fatigue~\cite{frisch2026complicated}.

\section{Conclusion}
%% =====================================================================
An oracle for meaning preservation is software and needs tests of its own.
Ours caught a repair that hid real substitutions and a builder that supplied the wrong cue, and its labels agree with human coders on two new sets of outputs.
With it, a human-written replication finds that bare keywords leave a default reading, that supplied speech-act cues help but are not always kept, and that some models change typed text.
Families, requirements, harness, oracle tests, stored outputs and analysis code are available at \url{https://github.com/1unarzDev/aac_voice_preservation}.

\section*{Ethics and Positionality}
No data were collected from AAC users; AACText is public under CC BY 4.0.
All annotators were adult non-authors recruited from the authors' university classmates, identified only by an ID; each consented and was reimbursed for their time (payment and food).
They labelled model outputs and audited items under a fixed codebook, blinded to model, condition and source.
Some also chose keywords for public AACText messages, and some wrote new sentence pairs, which they released under CC~BY~4.0 with recorded consent.
No personal or health data were collected.
As independent researchers, we did not seek an institutional review determination.
No AAC users, speech-language pathologists or autistic advisers took part in this work, which limits it: every requirement here is our assumption about what matters, not a finding about what AAC users want, and the requirements should be revisited with AAC users before any deployment decision.
We drew on writing by and with autistic and AAC-using adults~\cite{zisk2019aac,donaldson2021everyone,frisch2026complicated} and on participatory-research and double-empathy work~\cite{nicolaidis2019aaspire,milton2012double} to avoid encoding a group style or deficit; that reading is not a substitute for participation.
We assume no \say{autistic style}, and our claims are limited to commitments explicit in the text.
