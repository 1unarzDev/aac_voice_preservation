\begin{abstract}
When a language model expands a person's AAC keywords into a sentence, that sentence is spoken as the person's own, so a dropped \say{not} puts words in their mouth.
Deciding whether an output kept the meaning needs a test oracle: an automated checker that is itself software and can be wrong.
We built a test harness with six traceable requirements and tested its oracle with standard software-testing methods, and the tests changed what the oracle can be trusted for.
Rule-seeded mutants suggested near-perfect sensitivity (\kof{orr_j_mut}), but mutants written without the oracle's lexicon and confirmed by people showed \pct{oi_all} overall and \pct{oi_nc} where its lexicon cannot see the change; a seeded-fault sweep found two components with no tests.
Its critical labels matched people on all \RES{tc_c_k} treated outputs that had a human consensus (of \RES{tc_items}).
Applied to four models on constructed and crowd-written messages, the tested oracle shows that bare keywords default to the affirmative (\pct{hwaff}) and that supplying the missing cue raises the intended speech act by \RES{hx_p1} points (exploratory); in a listener study with adults, not AAC users, the cue raised the intended reading from \pct{ls_bare} to \pct{ls_chip}.
Passing the oracle is necessary but not sufficient: writers rejected \pct{wm_right_rej} of the outputs it passed, and two paid design consultants who use AAC asked for a person-approval requirement.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%% =====================================================================
Some people who use augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) compose a message from a few keywords, because each selection costs time and effort~\cite{valencia2023less,cai2024speakfaster}.
A language model then expands the keywords into a sentence~\cite{shen2022kwickchat,cai2024speakfaster}, which is spoken as the person's own words.
Typing \kw{supervisor read} might mean \say{The supervisor read} or \say{The supervisor did not read} (Fig.~\ref{fig:gallery}).
Whether the model erred depends on what the user supplied, which a text-similarity score cannot know, so a dedicated checker, a \emph{test oracle}, must decide.

This paper asks which output changes count as failures, given exactly what the user supplied, and how to test the checker that decides.
The second question is the software-engineering core.
Our checker is itself an LLM pipeline, that is, software with faults of its own, and every result passes through it, so we test it the way we would test any other software.
We contribute
(1)~a harness with six requirements, R1--R6, each with a stated source and, where the oracle can see it, an executable test (\S\ref{sec:harness});
(2)~tests of the oracle that changed what it is trusted for: independent mutants that exposed what rule-seeded mutants hid, two defects in our own harness turned into regression tests, and a seeded-fault sweep that found two untested components (\S\ref{sec:oracle});
(3)~evidence that the tested oracle finds real, model-specific behavior on human-written pairs, a machine-written confirmation set and a listener study (\S\ref{sec:results}); and
(4)~a requirements review by two paid design consultants who use AAC (not a sample), with each change traced (\S\ref{sec:consult}).
Table~\ref{tab:status} sorts the evidence into what people judged, what machines scored and what is exploratory.
The harness and stored outputs are released; the harness is for developers, not for assessing people who use AAC or their messages.

\section{Requirements and Harness}
\label{sec:harness}
%% =====================================================================
\subsection{Test items and input conditions}
Each test item is a \emph{minimal pair}: two messages that share their content words and differ in one critical detail, such as negation, who acts, certainty, the speech act (question, request or statement), politeness, or time and quantity.
Each message reaches the model in five \emph{input conditions}, which differ in how much the user supplied (Fig.~\ref{fig:gallery}).
The model may see only the keywords (\emph{bare}); the keywords plus one neutral word, as a control for length (\emph{+word}); the keywords plus a \emph{cue chip} that states the critical detail, such as \chip{NOT} (\emph{+chip}); chips for every detail (\emph{+all}); or the whole typed sentence (\emph{full}).
Across the human-written polarity pairs, \RES{hwe_pol_fix}\% of the \RES{hwe_pol_fix_n} wrong bare outputs are right once the chip is added.
A chip is a probe of whether supplied information is used, not a proposed interface: on a real device it costs a selection and must be learned.
A plain word such as \kw{not} can carry the same cue, and we test both.

\subsection{Requirements}
Table~\ref{tab:trace} lists six requirements, each with its source and its test.
The two central verdicts are a \emph{Violation}, when the output drops a value the user supplied (R1, R2), and an \emph{Assumption}, when the output commits to a value the input left open (R3).
A similarity score cannot draw this line: it would charge the model for what the interface omitted, and it would pass an added \say{please} on a typed sentence.
R1--R5 are our proposal, derived from the task and from defects we met; two paid design consultants who use AAC then asked for R6 and refined the others (\S\ref{sec:consult}).
R2 encodes a policy, keep typed wording unless the person asks for a rewrite and sees it first, which protects authorship; since some people prefer rewriting, it is a policy to test, not a recommendation.

\subsection{How a test runs}
Fig.~\ref{fig:harness} shows the pipeline.
A validator first checks that the displayed chips match the source (R5), and a model writes a sentence.
An LLM extractor (gpt-6.1-sol) reads only that sentence and answers questions about its details.
Two differently worded extractor prompts answer independently; when they disagree on the critical detail, a tie-breaker from another model family (a Claude Sonnet 5.5 session shown only the sentence and the question, ruling after outcomes were known) decides or leaves it unknown.
Code, not an LLM, then turns the labels into verdicts, so changing a rule re-scores every stored output without calling a model.
The extractor comes from the same family as one tested model (gpt-6.1-terra) and might favour its outputs~\cite{zheng2023judging}; the human checks in \S\ref{sec:oracle} are the mitigation.
The oracle checks only a fixed set of explicit details; it does not judge fluency, referents or whether anyone would accept the sentence.

\subsection{Data and models}
All items are authored or crowd-written, not messages from people who use AAC.
The \emph{human-written pairs} (primary) are \RES{hw_nkept} pairs kept of \RES{hw_ncand}, written by \RES{hw_nwriters} non-author writers, and each passed three blind checks; a second round added speech-act pairs from more writers.
The \emph{confirmation set} has \RES{wc_n_fam} fresh machine-written pairs on three details, with three contrasts fixed in a timestamped plan before generation, and the \emph{development set} has \RES{n_fam_test} authored pairs used to build the prompts and codebook.
Corpus transfer uses crowd-written messages from AACText~\cite{vertanen2011imagination}.
Four models run with one fixed prompt: Qwen2.5-7B, Llama-3.1-8B and Gemma-3-4B (local, 4-bit) and the API model gpt-6.1-terra; breadth checks add two larger open models (\RES{mb_nmod} models from \RES{mb_nfam} families).
Intervals are 95\% bootstrap intervals clustered by writer or item unless labelled; proportions use Wilson intervals.

\section{Testing the Oracle}
\label{sec:oracle}
%% =====================================================================
Every result is only as good as the checker, so we test it as software, following the test-oracle literature~\cite{weyuker1982nontestable,barr2015oracle} and mutation analysis~\cite{jia2011mutation}: an allowed change must pass and a forbidden one must fail.
An \emph{identity test} feeds a source sentence back as its own output; it must pass.
A \emph{mutant} is a copy of an output with one detail changed, such as a removed \say{not}; it must fail.
A \emph{control} is a copy with only a surface edit (case, punctuation, contraction); it must pass.
A \emph{human gate} compares the extractor's labels with human coders.
\emph{Development} items built the codebook and prompts; \emph{held-out} items did not.

\subsection{Two defects, two regression tests}
While we built the harness, the identity test failed on one sentence, \say{Yes, you can borrow my car this weekend.}, which the extractor coded as a statement instead of the intended acceptance: the model had done its job, and the checker demanded a distinction the text need not force (Defect~A).
The obvious repair, accepting a statement for an acceptance target, passed the identity test but broke the next one: the relaxed oracle missed both mutants that turned the acceptance into a plain statement (\say{You borrowed my car this weekend.}), whereas the strict rule caught all \RES{or_new_mut_det_raw_k} challenge mutants.
Removing a false alarm had removed fault sensitivity.
We kept the relaxation in production, because the strict rule fails an unchanged source, and contained it instead: both refusal/acceptance pairs are quarantined from every primary result, and acceptance mutants now guard the rule.
One sentence is a demonstration, not a rate.

In Defect~B, our input builder silently fell back to a wrong chip for \RES{wb_conflicts} of \RES{wb_msgs_restored} AACText messages, for example \chip{MAYBE} on a plain assertion; the models followed the chip, so a fidelity score would have blamed them.
The R5 validator, which reads only the rendered input, now rejects all \RES{r5_conf} before generation and found no conflicts in all \RES{t9_inputs} chip inputs of our studies.

\subsection{Seeded faults found two untested components}
The defects were found by accident, so we then seeded faults on purpose: \RES{sf_n} one-line mutants of the harness's own code in \RES{sf_ncls} classes (Table~\ref{tab:matrix}), each run against the whole test suite.
The first round killed \RES{sf_r1_k} (\pct{sf_r1}).
The survivors clustered: judge post-processing (\RES{sf_k3_r1} of \RES{sf_k3_n} killed) and the input builder (\RES{sf_k4_r1} of \RES{sf_k4_n}) had no test that ran their code on stored data, including faults that silently relabel outputs.
We added two test files, a replay of about 41,000 stored judgments and a golden rebuild of stored requests, and all \RES{sf_n} mutants were then killed.
This is a coverage score on a fixed list, not a fault-detection rate, because the new tests were written after we saw the survivors.

\subsection{Rule-seeded mutants overstated sensitivity}
Table~\ref{tab:oracle} collects the oracle evidence.
On authored sentences the oracle passed all \RES{or_identity_fa_den} identity tests and caught all \RES{or_mut_det_den} hand-written mutants.
On a challenge set whose expected outcomes were fixed in advance, the production rule caught \kof{or_new_mut_det} mutants with no false alarms, passing its gate with zero margin because of Defect~A's relaxation.
We then seeded one-detail mutants into \RES{orr_j_mut_den} real stored outputs with rule-based operators: the oracle caught \RES{orr_j_mut_k}, with no false alarms among \RES{orr_nctl} controls.
But the operators mirror the extractor's own cue words, so this score is near-circular; a plain word list scores \RES{orr_s_mut_k}.

To break the circle, we wrote \RES{oi_all_n} mutants without the oracle's lexicon and had two annotators confirm each.
The oracle caught \pct{oi2_ja} of them, and only \pct{oi2_jb} (Wilson \cip{oi_nc}) of the \RES{oi_nc_n} whose change its lexicon cannot see (surface baselines: \pct{oi_surf} and \pct{oi_ovl}), with \kn{oi_fa} false alarms on controls.
It is weak on \say{fail to}, \say{refuse to} and embedded roles.
An NLI model and a Claude relabel caught more of the lexicon-blind mutants (\pct{oi2_nb} and \pct{oi2_cb}), but both saw the original sentence, which the oracle does not, and Claude is not independent of our tooling, so this is a sensitivity analysis.
The drop from near-perfect to \pct{oi2_jb} is the point of testing the oracle: it tells developers which changes the checker can be trusted to catch.

\subsection{The oracle against people}
On outputs nobody edited, human coders are the reference.
On two held-out sets, the oracle's violation verdicts had \pct{nat_ho_viol_p} precision and \pct{nat_ho_viol_r} recall (\RES{nat_ho_viol_npos} violations), and its preservation-failure verdicts \pct{nat_ho_pres_p} and \pct{nat_ho_pres_r}.
On \RES{tc_items} outputs of cue, retry and rewrite conditions, its critical label equalled the human consensus on all \kof{tc_c} outputs with a consensus; the added-content label is the weakest ($\kappa=\RES{tc_add_kappa}$).
Extractor labels pass the pre-set gate ($\kappa\geq0.6$) on all \RES{human_gate_n} fields against two coders, and critical $\kappa$ stays at or above \RES{t7e_k} on two later sets.
People sided with the tie-breaker in \pct{p19_arb} of \RES{p19_arb_n} codings.
Relabelling all \RES{rl_units} units with a second family (Claude) agreed with people on \pct{rl_c_acc} (the GPT extractor: \pct{rl_g_acc}) and moved the speech-act cue gain from \RES{rl_p1_gpt} to \RES{rl_p1} points; the same family also served as tie-breaker and assisted the project, so this is a second opinion, not an independent check.

\subsection{Necessary, not sufficient}
The oracle checks a fixed set of explicit values, so passing it is necessary for a faithful sentence, not sufficient.
Writers judged \RES{p18_acc_n} outputs generated from their own pairs and accepted \RES{p18_acc_k} as written.
Of \RES{wm_right_rej_den} outputs the oracle passed, writers said \RES{wm_right_rej_k} (\pct{wm_right_rej}) were not the version they meant, mostly because words from their sentence were missing (\pct{wm_dropped}) or the event or role changed (\pct{wm_subst}), not because content was added.
An output the oracle passes therefore still needs the person's approval (R6), a cue the person controls, and exact typed words for typed sentences (R2).
Both speech-language pathologists (SLPs) we consulted asked that relations (who does what) be preserved, and R4 now says so.
Event and role structure is a missing requirement, not a fault of the oracle.
The cheapest test for it, T1, flags a \chip{TELL} input that yields a form of \kw{tell} absent from the keywords: \pct{t1} of such outputs on the human-written pairs do, against \pct{t1_ctrl} of bare outputs; T1 is lexical and not yet validated by people.

\subsection{Confirmation set}
All three contrasts have the same sign on the \RES{wc_n_fam} fresh pairs, and every interval excludes zero (Table~\ref{tab:primary}).
Two qualifications apply.
\RES{wc_prefreeze} cached requests predate the final item freeze, and the speech-act gain relies on tie-breaker rulings made after outcomes were known; with those labels masked it is \RES{wcp1_um} points.
The plan was timestamped in a private repository; the public hash release postdates the freeze and does not certify it.
The \chip{?} chip is the weakest: alone it failed \RES{wc_qfail_k} of \RES{wc_qfail_n} times, mostly because \say{Can you\ldots?} was coded as a request; coding it otherwise lowers the gain from \RES{cb_wc_base} to \RES{cb_wc_q} points without changing any decision, so the size depends on the codebook and the sign does not.

\subsection{Listeners}
Twenty adults, not AAC users or communication partners, each read one model sentence and chose which of two meanings the person intended (or neither) and how they would respond.
With bare keywords they chose the intended meaning for \pct{ls_bare} of sentences, and with a cue chip for \pct{ls_chip} (+\RES{ls_diff} points [\RES{ls_lo}, \RES{ls_hi}]).
They chose the response that fits the opposite meaning for \pct{ls_cq_bare} of bare and \pct{ls_cq_chip} of cue sentences (secondary).
Outright misreadings, the primary measure, were rare and did not differ reliably (\RES{ls_rd} per 1,000 [\RES{ls_rd_lo}, \RES{ls_rd_hi}]; borderline at best under crossed listener-by-item models); most bare sentences were judged unclear (\pct{ls_un_bare}).

\section{Related Work}
%% =====================================================================
\subsection{Test oracles, metamorphic testing and requirements for ML}
Whether an output is correct is the oracle problem~\cite{barr2015oracle}; partial or pseudo oracles are standard~\cite{weyuker1982nontestable}, and metamorphic testing checks relations between runs~\cite{chen2018metamorphic}.
Mutation analysis measures a test suite by whether it detects seeded faults~\cite{jia2011mutation}; we apply it to the oracle, not the system.
Requirements engineering for ML calls for explicit, testable requirements~\cite{vogelsang2019re4ml}; R1--R6 are one such set, with sources and tests stated.

\subsection{Testing LLM systems}
CheckList organizes behavioral tests~\cite{ribeiro2020checklist} and Cho et al.\ catalogue metamorphic relations~\cite{cho2025metamorphic}; we claim no new technique.
LLM judges show position, verbosity and self-preference biases~\cite{zheng2023judging}, and preservation metrics look valid when test data confound style change with content errors~\cite{pauli2025stylegap}; our identity and mutant tests apply that idea to a checker.

\subsection{Preservation and LLMs in AAC}
Qu et al.\ evaluate speech-act retention in style transfer~\cite{qu2023conditioning}, and rewriting can keep semantic similarity while erasing pragmatic markers~\cite{navneet2026voice}.
AAC systems studied with users expose user-side controls over context or stance~\cite{valencia2023less,valencia2024compa}; a Lacosse et al.\ preprint keeps a short list of bracketed function words, including \say{not}~\cite{lacosse2026cognitivesteering}; Choudhury et al.\ test whether affect cues reach the output~\cite{choudhury2025alignment}.
We add a conditional specification of which commitments are testable under each input, and tests of the oracle that checks them.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}
%% =====================================================================
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item \emph{Construct.} Labels are coarse, explicit categories; referents, negation scope, certainty degree and event structure are only partly covered (\S\ref{sec:oracle}), and passing the checker is weaker than keeping the typed wording.
\item \emph{Oracle.} The main limitation: all rates depend on an LLM extractor and tie-breaker, and the extractor shares a model family with one generator; a Claude re-label (above) answers this for the speech-act cue gain, but the same family also arbitrated and assisted. The defect trace has $n=2$ defects found by our own process, and the sweep's \RES{sf_r2_k}/\RES{sf_den} covers mutants that informed the new tests: both show that the process works, not that it finds defects we did not anticipate.
\item \emph{External.} \RES{mb_nmod} models from \RES{mb_nfam} families (open models at most 14B, 4-bit), one prompt, English, constructed and crowd-written rather than messages from people who use AAC; effects are model-specific, and zero observed events do not establish zero risk.
\item \emph{Use.} No outside developer has used the harness yet. The SLP raters and the design consultants are two people each and neither establishes what people who use AAC in general want; selection counts omit reading, navigation and fatigue~\cite{frisch2026complicated}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
%% =====================================================================
An oracle for meaning preservation is software and needs tests of its own.
Testing ours changed what we trust it for: it caught a repair that hid real substitutions and a builder that supplied the wrong chip; mutants written without its lexicon, a second model family and an NLI model located where it is weak; and a seeded-fault sweep found two components with no tests, which now have them.
Its critical labels agree with human coders on three sets of outputs, including all \kof{tc_c} treated outputs with a human consensus.
With it, human-written pairs show that bare keywords leave a default reading, that supplying the missing cue helped adult readers of stored sentences pick the intended reading, and that some models change typed text.
Passing the oracle is necessary but not sufficient: event and role structure is a requirement it does not check, so review by two design consultants and two SLP raters added R6 and relations in R4, and a lexical regression test (T1) awaits human validation.
Families, requirements, harness, oracle tests, stored outputs and analysis code are available at \url{https://github.com/1unarzDev/aac_voice_preservation}.

\section*{Ethics and Positionality}
No data about people who use AAC were collected; AACText is public under CC BY 4.0.
All annotators were adult non-authors, classmates of the authors from a university (we list no affiliation because the work was not run through an institution); each consented, was identified only by an ID, and was reimbursed for their time (payment and food).
They labelled model outputs and audited items under a fixed codebook, blind to model, condition and source; some also chose keywords for public AACText messages or wrote new sentence pairs, released under CC~BY~4.0 with recorded consent.
No personal or health data were collected, and as independent researchers we did not seek an institutional review determination.
Two speech-language pathologists (paid, anonymous expert annotators of study materials) and two paid design consultants (who reviewed requirements and examples) worked under written agreements; we collected no data about their experiences, demographics or health and describe them only by role, and the paraphrases in Table~\ref{tab:consult} have not been member-checked.
We drew on writing by and with autistic and AAC-using adults~\cite{zisk2019aac,donaldson2021everyone,frisch2026complicated} and on participatory-research and double-empathy work~\cite{nicolaidis2019aaspire,milton2012double} to avoid encoding a group style or deficit; our claims are limited to commitments explicit in the text.
\emph{Positionality.} The authors are two independent researchers; none of us reports using AAC, and we do not draw on lived AAC experience.
No person who uses AAC took part as a participant or co-author, so what people who use AAC want, accept or benefit from is untested here; our claims concern the tests, not the people.
