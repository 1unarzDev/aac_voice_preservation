\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

A person who communicates by selecting a few words can now ask a language model to turn them into a sentence. For a writer who selects words slowly this promises a large gain in speed, and it is the premise of a line of systems that expand abbreviations, keywords and partial phrases into full utterances \cite{shen2022kwickchat,cai2022abbrev,cai2024speakfaster}. It also changes what a message is. The words the writer selects are the writer's message in compressed form, and the model fills in everything they leave open. Whether the filled-in sentence is the sentence the writer meant is a property of the model's defaults, not of its fluency. Unusual, blunt, short and incomplete sentences are legitimate messages, so a system that makes them smoother has changed them; this is why we study drafts that a system only proposes and the writer approves.

\paragraph{Lead result.}
We asked \jmpOneEightWriters{} writers to rate model drafts of their own keywords against the sentences they had intended. A one-token cue added to the keywords raised the share of drafts judged to be the intended version from \jmpOneEightMatchBare{}\% to \jmpOneEightMatchChip{}\% (\jmpOneEightCueGain{} points [\jmpOneEightCueGainLo{}, \jmpOneEightCueGainHi{}]; \jmpOneEightAcceptN{} drafts, \jmwmModels{} models). Yet writers accepted only \jmpOneEightAcceptYesK{} of the \jmpOneEightAcceptN{} drafts as written, and \jmpOneEightAcceptYesSmall{}\% as written or with a small edit. The shortfall is not added content (\jmwmAdded{}\% of primary reasons). Among drafts whose critical commitment an automatic judge marked correct, the main reasons were misresolved events and roles (\jmwmRightSubst{}\%) and detail lost in compression (\jmwmRightDropped{}\%). Preserving the critical commitment is therefore a necessary filter and not the objective. The objective is to cover the writer's whole intended message with drafts that the writer approves before anything is spoken. This single result organises the paper; the remaining studies establish the filter (a missing cue is the main source of silent reversals, and a cue repairs them), check the instruments that scored it, and bound what asking and guarding add.

\paragraph{Who the data come from.}
Every human datum in this paper comes from adult non-author university classmates (the writers and coders) and from two speech-language pathologists. None is an AAC user, and the sentences the writers intended were written for this study. Our central claim is therefore \emph{writer-intent agreement among non-AAC adults}. Two paid design consultants who use AAC reviewed the requirement list; we report only what changed in it, and we treat nothing they said as data. We do not claim benefit to AAC users; Section~\ref{sec:limitations} states the limits once.

\paragraph{What is new relative to our earlier papers.}
Two shorter papers from this project, an archival paper and a non-archival workshop paper \cite{concurrent_se4all,concurrent_caleai}, report the deletion-based guard on constructed families (speech-act joint pass \jmgtJointP{}\% to \jmgtJointG{}\% on \jmgtFamilies{} fresh families) and the discovery-set cue result. We restate those results in shortened form and cite them; the guard numbers appear in this paper only in Section~\ref{sec:guard}, where they are marked as restated. New here are the human-written pair replication (\jmhwWritersTotal{} writers), the writer-intent study and whole-message analysis, the clinician severity weights, the blind repair-usability audit, the human check of the arbiter, the same-items profile and the ask-policy comparison. The same-items speech-act figures (\jmfOneActRestoreChip{}\% to \jmfOneActRestoreChipG{}\%, \jmfOneUnits{} elicitation units) differ from the guard figures above because they come from a different item set, a different baseline (cue present) and three models.

\paragraph{Why the cue matters.}
Consider one stored case. A writer's intended sentence was \emph{``The supervisor does not read my schedule.''} The keywords were \texttt{supervisor read}. A local model returned \emph{``The supervisor read.''}, fluent and the opposite of the message; with the cue \texttt{[NOT]} added it returned \emph{``The supervisor did not read.''} This is a rule-selected best-case illustration and not a typical draw: it is the first, in a fixed hash order, of \jmexPolFamilies{} polarity families in which the bare output committed to the wrong polarity and the cue output to the right one. Because the rule conditions on a wrong bare output, the cue's \jmexPolChipMatch{}\% [\jmexPolChipMatchLo{}, \jmexPolChipMatchHi{}] success on those \jmexPolChipMatchN{} rows is a best case. The rate at which bare outputs were wrong (\jmexPolWrong{}\% of \jmexPolWrongN{} rows from \jmexPolWriters{} writers) is a design ceiling of a contrast whose two variants share one bare input, not a model finding; the informative quantity is the gain from the cue.

\paragraph{Supporting results.}
The default is systematic in the minimal-pair setting: with bare keywords models chose the affirmative reading in at least \jmbreadthMinAff{}\% of inputs across \jmbreadthModels{} models, and without the prompt legend the share stays at \jmlegendNoLegendAff{}\% (four models). The bare input is built to be ambiguous between the two variants, so the affirmative share is partly a property of that design; it points in the direction Chen et al.\ report for negative commonsense facts \cite{chen2023say}, which we do not claim as new. What we add is its price on the same \jmfOneUnits{} items (three models): polarity reversals fall from \jmfOneRevPolBare{} to \jmfOneRevPolChip{} per 1,000 messages with the cue. Weighted by what two speech-language pathologists judged serious, the cue lowers the failure rate from \jmslpWCueBare{}\% to \jmslpWCueChip{}\%; with two raters this is an expert review, not an estimate for a population. A blind audit by three classmate raters per item of guard repairs was inconclusive: \jmpOneSevenRepUsable{}\% of repairs were usable against \jmpOneSevenCtlUsable{}\% of controls, a difference of \jmpOneSevenDiffUsable{} points [\jmpOneSevenDiffUsableLo{}, \jmpOneSevenDiffUsableHi{}], so a usability cost is neither shown nor excluded. Automatic judges agreed with the writers only weakly ($\kappa$ \jmpOneEightKappa{}). The arbiter that resolves judge disagreements matched human coders in \jmpOneNineAgreeArb{}\% of the \jmpOneNineCodings{} committed codings on disputed units (a check with \jmpOneNineSensUnsure{}\% of codings excluded as unsure or blank; Section~\ref{sec:m-arbiter}). Last, ask-when-uncertain policies did not beat asking at random at the same number of taps under an arithmetic model; no person was asked.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A writer-intent result: a one-token cue raises the intended-version match from \jmpOneEightMatchBare{}\% to \jmpOneEightMatchChip{}\%, yet one draft in \jmpOneEightAcceptN{} is accepted as written, and the shortfall is compression loss and misresolved events and roles, not added content.
\item A commitment-preservation harness with writer-written minimal pairs, controlled cue channels, and a per-dimension default priced per 1,000 messages, with its denominators stated.
\item An assessment of the instruments (judges, arbiter, clinician weights, repair audit) with the exclusions and the dependence between codings reported.
\item Requirement changes from two design consultants (drafts never spoken without approval, an exact-copy default, differing drafts shown) and bounded negative results on ask policies, model uncertainty and partner context.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Language models for AAC text entry and expansion.}
Prediction for AAC has long traded keystrokes for speed. Recent systems use large language models to expand compressed input. Cai et al.\ expand first-letter abbreviations in context, reaching exact expansion of most phrases up to a stated abbreviation length and showing that a single prior turn more than doubles accuracy \cite{cai2022abbrev}. SpeakFaster extends this with keyword and mask editing for eye-gaze typists with ALS and evaluates keystroke savings in simulation, in a pilot with non-AAC participants and with eye-gaze typists \cite{cai2024speakfaster}. KWickChat generates context-aware sentences from bags of keywords for AAC dialogue \cite{shen2022kwickchat}. The measures in this literature are motor savings, exact match or user-rated quality. Valencia et al.\ show why that is not enough: AAC users who tested live model suggestions judged them able to save time and effort, but found it important that phrases reflect their own communication style and preferences \cite{valencia2023less}. Work on affective sentence generation tests whether an emotion cue reaches the output \cite{choudhury2025alignment}, and design probes with people with aphasia examine how AI-driven AAC meets complex communication needs \cite{mao2025designprobes}. None of these studies, as far as we found, ask whether a specific commitment the writer did not type (a negation, a sentence type, an actor) is silently overwritten by a model default, or price that failure per message.

\paragraph{Acceptance, abandonment and review burden.}
Assistive technology is abandoned at a high rate when it does not match what the person needs or wants to do \cite{phillips1993abandonment}, and clinicians report that the fit between a system and its user's communication goals decides whether AAC succeeds or is dropped \cite{johnson2006abandonment}. Text prediction adds a review burden: every suggestion must be read and judged, and predictive text shapes what people write \cite{arnold2020predictable}. Valencia et al.\ report that AAC users want suggestions to reflect their own style \cite{valencia2023less}. These findings make the cost of a draft that is almost, but not quite, the intended message a first-order concern, and are why we measure acceptance as written and with a small edit instead of match alone. The tradition of ``nothing about us without us'' \cite{charlton1998nothing} is also why we separate what classmates and clinicians can tell us from what only AAC users can.

\paragraph{Faithfulness and positivity bias.}
Chen et al.\ show that language models produce sentences that are too positive about negative commonsense knowledge, including in a constrained keywords-to-sentence task, and attribute the effect to negation reporting bias and statistical shortcuts \cite{chen2023say}. Their task scores generated sentences against factual truth and is not set in AAC. We treat their result as the prior for polarity and contribute three things it does not contain: the human base rate against which the default is measured, a per-dimension profile that extends beyond negation to politeness, speech act, actor and certainty, and the price per 1,000 messages. The direction of a default also depends on the task: Braun finds that language models lean toward answering ``no'' on agreement tasks, unlike the acquiescence typical of people \cite{braun2025acquiescence}. This is a reason to profile each dimension and not to assume one direction for all of them, which is how we proceed. For testing behavior beyond accuracy, we use the logic of behavioral testing with minimal pairs \cite{ribeiro2020checklist}.

\paragraph{Clarification, cues and when to ask.}
Interactive systems can resolve ambiguity by asking. Zhang and Choi frame the decision as a trade-off between the user's preference for speed and for care, and estimate the entropy over user intents to decide when clarification is worth its cost \cite{zhang2025clarify}. Conf-Gen brings conformal risk control to generative models and includes a guarantee that a chat system has asked enough clarifying questions \cite{loaizaganem2026confgen}. These works treat a question as a cost the user bears, which in AAC is a physical cost per selection. We take the cheapest form of that interaction, a one-token cue the writer may supply before generation, and compare it against ask policies on the same items. % CITE-NEEDED: models recognize ambiguity but seldom ask (arXiv 2605.25284 and AskBench arXiv 2602.11199 were seen only as search snippets, not opened).

\paragraph{AI-mediated communication and authorship.}
Hancock, Naaman and Levy define AI-mediated communication as interpersonal communication in which an intelligent agent operates on behalf of a communicator by modifying, augmenting or generating messages \cite{hancock2020aimc}. AAC expansion is a case where the agent generates the message from a specification, which makes authorship an evaluation question and not only an ethical one. Jakesch et al.\ show in an online experiment that a writing assistant built to argue one side shifted the opinions that participants expressed and later reported \cite{jakesch2023opinionated}, and Valencia et al.\ report that AAC users want suggested phrases to be theirs \cite{valencia2023less}. These findings motivate an approval gate: a draft is the writer's message only if the writer says it is. % CITE-NEEDED: AAC-specific authorship and agency work (Valencia et al. 2020, Griffiths et al. 2025) is in refs.bib or the literature notes but was not re-opened for this section.

\paragraph{Validity of the evaluation instrument.}
Language-model judges are widely used and have known position, verbosity and self-enhancement biases \cite{zheng2023judging}; prediction-powered inference gives a way to correct their error with a small human-labelled sample \cite{angelopoulos2023ppi}. In AAC, Frisch et al.\ argue that technical, user-mediated and functional evaluation must be combined, and call for measuring alignment with the writer's intent \cite{frisch2026complicated}. We measure it: against the writers' own intended sentences, against clinicians' severity ratings and against a human-validated arbiter, and we treat the resulting agreement as a finding about the instrument.
