Q: How important is R1?
A: Important

Q: Is anything wrong with R1, or missing from it?
A: Keep the meaning of a selected button and its scope. NOT must attach to the intended action, not simply appear somewhere. Apply the same promise when a meaning is entered as an ordinary word. If two selections conflict, ask rather than silently choosing one.

Q: How important is R2?
A: Important

Q: Is anything wrong with R2, or missing from it?
A: Keep an exact-copy mode as the default for complete typed sentences. Rewriting should require an explicit request, a visible proposed change, and approval before speech. The bad example here uses keywords and PLAIN, so it does not actually illustrate changing a whole typed sentence; add a typed-sentence example.

Q: How important is R3?
A: Important

Q: Is anything wrong with R3, or missing from it?
A: Show the particular unresolved choice, such as who acts or whether the message asks or tells. Let the person select an interpretation, keep the short wording, or cancel. Do not force a full-sentence choice or silently learn a permanent preference from a single answer. Extra steps are justified when they protect meaning, but they should be brief and reversible.

Q: How important is R4?
A: Important

Q: Is anything wrong with R4, or missing from it?
A: Preserve every compatible meaning already selected, rather than preserving the previous system sentence merely because it looked fluent. If a new selection deliberately corrects an earlier one, show that change for approval. The examples need clearer wording about which parts were confirmed and which were only guessed.

Q: How important is R5?
A: Important

Q: Is anything wrong with R5, or missing from it?
A: Check what the test input actually communicates before judging the output. Separate a faulty meaning button from a model ignoring a correct button. Test plain words and buttons with equivalent content and equivalent instructions; the two design mockups currently produce different event times and cannot establish which input style is better.

Q: Is there a rule we forgot? What would you add?
A: Add a rule that the person decides when a draft is spoken. A generated sentence must remain a draft until approved, with a clear way to hear or inspect it privately, change it, or cancel. Show added meaning where it occurs. Keep the original words available and offer direct speech of those words even when the system cannot confidently expand them.

Q: What should a system never change without asking?
A: Do not change agreement or refusal, the scope of not, who acts or receives an action, ownership, permission, consent, urgency, certainty, numbers, names, event time, or an explicit tone choice without asking. Do not invent reasons, feelings, promises, destinations, or reported conversations. A direct boundary must not become a polite suggestion by default.

Q: Which is easier to imagine using: a button or a plain word?
A: The button (chip) is easier to imagine

Q: Why?
A: For this design, an explicit button makes a meaning choice visible and keeps a control such as statement separate from message content. The selected state must be easy to inspect, remove, and reach using the same access method as the words. Plain words should remain an equivalent option. This is a design preference, not evidence that buttons take fewer selections; test the actual navigation and scanning order.

Q: Anything else about the design or these pages?
A: The person should control the final message, including the option to keep an unusual, blunt, short, or incomplete sentence. A warning should explain the specific uncertainty and let the person choose; it should not become a demand to sound polite or typical. Compare access costs on a working prototype rather than implying that either mockup is easier. Keep control labels separate from words that could be spoken, and ensure that support for correction does not remove the option to speak the original selection.
