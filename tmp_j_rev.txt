     A person who types a few keywords and lets a language model write the sentence needs the sentence to be the one they meant. We
8    asked 20 classmate writers, none of them AAC users, to rate model drafts of their own keywords against the sentences they had
9    intended (200 drafts, 4 models). A one-token cue raised the share of drafts judged to be the intended version from 11.2% to 39.2%, and
10   on 332 further items (three models) it cut polarity reversals from 23.8 to 1.6 per 1,000 messages. Preserving the critical commitment is
11   nonetheless necessary and not sufficient: writers accepted 1 of 200 drafts as written and 40.0% as written or with a small edit, and
12
     the shortfall came from misresolved events and roles and detail lost in compression, not added content (0.0% of primary reasons).
13
     Automatic judges agreed with the writers only weakly (𝜅 0.24), so each result is reported against a human check, with two clinician
14
15
     raters and an inconclusive repair-usability audit marked as thin; access-method, tap and reading costs were not studied, and two paid
16   design consultants who use AAC shaped the requirements only. We therefore propose treating commitment preservation as a filter
17   and measuring how much of the writer’s whole intended message a set of approval-gated drafts covers.

24   1   Introduction
25
     A person who communicates by selecting a few words can now ask a language model to turn them into a sentence.
26
27   For a writer who selects words slowly this promises a large gain in speed, and it is the premise of a line of systems
28   that expand abbreviations, keywords and partial phrases into full utterances [7, 8, 21]. It also changes what a message
29   is. The words the writer selects are the writer’s message in compressed form, and the model fills in everything they
30
     leave open. Whether the filled-in sentence is the sentence the writer meant is a property of the model’s defaults, not
31
32   of its fluency. Unusual, blunt, short and incomplete sentences are legitimate messages, so a system that makes them
33   smoother has changed them; this is why we study drafts that a system only proposes and the writer approves.
34
35       Lead result. We asked 20 writers to rate model drafts of their own keywords against the sentences they had intended.
36   A one-token cue added to the keywords raised the share of drafts judged to be the intended version from 11.2% to 39.2%
37
     (+28.0 points [+14.8, +42.0]; 200 drafts, 4 models). Yet writers accepted only 1 of the 200 drafts as written, and 40.0% as
38
39
     written or with a small edit. The shortfall is not added content (0.0% of primary reasons). Among drafts whose critical
40   commitment an automatic judge marked correct, the main reasons were misresolved events and roles (48.8%) and detail
41   lost in compression (45.7%). Preserving the critical commitment is therefore a necessary filter and not the objective. The
42
     objective is to cover the writer’s whole intended message with drafts that the writer approves before anything is spoken.
43
44
     This single result organises the paper; the remaining studies establish the filter (a missing cue is the main source of
45   silent reversals, and a cue repairs them), check the instruments that scored it, and bound what asking and guarding add.
46

67        Why the cue matters. Consider one stored case. A writer’s intended sentence was “The supervisor does not read my
68
      schedule.” The keywords were supervisor read. A local model returned “The supervisor read.”, fluent and the opposite
69
70    of the message; with the cue [NOT] added it returned “The supervisor did not read.” This is a rule-selected best-case
71    illustration and not a typical draw: it is the first, in a fixed hash order, of 104 polarity families in which the bare output
72    committed to the wrong polarity and the cue output to the right one. Because the rule conditions on a wrong bare
73
      output, the cue’s 99.3% [98.6, 99.8] success on those 414 rows is a best case. The rate at which bare outputs were wrong
74
75    (49.8% of 832 rows from 7 writers) is a design ceiling of a contrast whose two variants share one bare input, not a model
76    finding; the informative quantity is the gain from the cue.

79

      2   Related Work
137       Language models for AAC text entry and expansion. Prediction for AAC has long traded keystrokes for speed. Recent
138
      systems use large language models to expand compressed input. Cai et al. expand first-letter abbreviations in context,
139
140
      reaching exact expansion of most phrases up to a stated abbreviation length and showing that a single prior turn more
141   than doubles accuracy [8]. SpeakFaster extends this with keyword and mask editing for eye-gaze typists with ALS
142   and evaluates keystroke savings in simulation, in a pilot with non-AAC participants and with eye-gaze typists [7].
143
      KWickChat generates context-aware sentences from bags of keywords for AAC dialogue [21]. The measures in this
144
145
      literature are motor savings, exact match or user-rated quality. Valencia et al. show why that is not enough: AAC users
146   who tested live model suggestions judged them able to save time and effort, but found it important that phrases reflect
147   their own communication style and preferences [22]. Work on affective sentence generation tests whether an emotion
148
      cue reaches the output [11], and design probes with people with aphasia examine how AI-driven AAC meets complex
149
150
      communication needs [18]. None of these studies, as far as we found, ask whether a specific commitment the writer
151   did not type (a negation, a sentence type, an actor) is silently overwritten by a model default, or price that failure per
152   message.
153
154       Acceptance, abandonment and review burden. Assistive technology is abandoned at a high rate when it does not
155
      match what the person needs or wants to do [19], and clinicians report that the fit between a system and its user’s
156
                                                                                                              Manuscript submitted to ACM
      4                                                                                                         Authors withheld

157   communication goals decides whether AAC succeeds or is dropped [16]. Text prediction adds a review burden: every
158
      suggestion must be read and judged, and predictive text shapes what people write [2]. Valencia et al. report that AAC
159
160
      users want suggestions to reflect their own style [22]. These findings make the cost of a draft that is almost, but not
161   quite, the intended message a first-order concern, and are why we measure acceptance as written and with a small edit
162   instead of match alone. The tradition of “nothing about us without us” [9] is also why we separate what classmates and
163
      clinicians can tell us from what only AAC users can.
164
165
166       Faithfulness and positivity bias. Chen et al. show that language models produce sentences that are too positive about
167
      negative commonsense knowledge, including in a constrained keywords-to-sentence task, and attribute the effect to
168
      negation reporting bias and statistical shortcuts [10]. Their task scores generated sentences against factual truth and is
169
170   not set in AAC. We treat their result as the prior for polarity and contribute three things it does not contain: the human
171   base rate against which the default is measured, a per-dimension profile that extends beyond negation to politeness,
172
      speech act, actor and certainty, and the price per 1,000 messages. The direction of a default also depends on the task:
173
      Braun finds that language models lean toward answering “no” on agreement tasks, unlike the acquiescence typical of
174
175   people [6]. This is a reason to profile each dimension and not to assume one direction for all of them, which is how we
176   proceed. For testing behavior beyond accuracy, we use the logic of behavioral testing with minimal pairs [20].
177
178
179       Clarification, cues and when to ask. Interactive systems can resolve ambiguity by asking. Zhang and Choi frame the
180   decision as a trade-off between the user’s preference for speed and for care, and estimate the entropy over user intents
181   to decide when clarification is worth its cost [24]. Conf-Gen brings conformal risk control to generative models and
182
      includes a guarantee that a chat system has asked enough clarifying questions [17]. These works treat a question as
183
184   a cost the user bears, which in AAC is a physical cost per selection. We take the cheapest form of that interaction, a
185   one-token cue the writer may supply before generation, and compare it against ask policies on the same items.
186
187
188
          AI-mediated communication and authorship. Hancock, Naaman and Levy define AI-mediated communication as
189   interpersonal communication in which an intelligent agent operates on behalf of a communicator by modifying,
190   augmenting or generating messages [13]. AAC expansion is a case where the agent generates the message from a
191
      specification, which makes authorship an evaluation question and not only an ethical one. Jakesch et al. show in an
192
193
      online experiment that a writing assistant built to argue one side shifted the opinions that participants expressed and
194   later reported [14], and Valencia et al. report that AAC users want suggested phrases to be theirs [22]. These findings
195   motivate an approval gate: a draft is the writer’s message only if the writer says it is.
196
197
198       Validity of the evaluation instrument. Language-model judges are widely used and have known position, verbosity
199   and self-enhancement biases [25]; prediction-powered inference gives a way to correct their error with a small human-
200
      labelled sample [1]. In AAC, Frisch et al. argue that technical, user-mediated and functional evaluation must be
201
      combined, and call for measuring alignment with the writer’s intent [12]. We measure it: against the writers’ own
202
203   intended sentences, against clinicians’ severity ratings and against a human-validated arbiter, and we treat the resulting
204   agreement as a finding about the instrument.

232   3     Method
233
234
      The method charges each wrong sentence to the party that could have prevented it. A message is rendered through
235   input channels that fix what the person supplied, a model writes a sentence, two extractor prompts label what the
236   sentence commits to, and deterministic code turns the labels into a verdict. The verdict is a function of the stored
237
      labels, the item specification and the channel, so a change of rule re-scores every stored output without a model call.
238
239
      This section defines the units and the attribution rule (Sections 3.1 and 3.2), describes the extractor and its arbiter
240   (Section 3.3), and then validates each link of the chain: the checker against seeded and unseeded faults (Section 3.4), the
241   extractor against human coders (Section 3.5) and the arbiter against human coders (Section 3.6). The writer-grounded
242
      and expert instruments that the later sections use are defined in Section 3.7, and estimation in Section 3.8. The harness
243
244
      follows the one first described in our concurrent archival and workshop papers [3, 4]; the description here is complete
245   for the results that follow.

247   3.1    Items, channels, models
248
249
          Families and dimensions. A family is a minimal pair of source sentences that share their content words and differ on
250   one critical dimension: polarity, agent (who acts), certainty, speech act (statement, question or request), politeness, or
251   time/quantity. The two members of a pair, variants A and B, are rendered from the same keywords, so the keywords
252
      alone cannot tell them apart. The extractor answers the family’s critical question for every output and five general
253
254
      questions (polarity, speech act, agent, certainty, politeness), and it answers an added-content question. These are the
255   seven fields that the human checks below validate.
256
257       Channels. Each variant is shown to the model through five channels that differ in how much of the message the
258   person supplied. Bare gives the keywords only. Non-critical adds one content word that does not bear on the critical
259
      dimension and so matches the length of a cue. Chip adds the critical value as a one-token cue such as [NOT], [?] or

306   3.2   Attribution by construction
307
      Attribution is a property of the design, not a judgment about each output. Let 𝐶 (𝑥) be the set of dimensions whose
308
      value input 𝑥 fixes: the chip or the typed sentence fixes the critical dimension in the chip, all-chips and full channels,
309
310   and nothing fixes it in the bare and non-critical channels. For an output 𝑦 with extracted value 𝐿𝑑 (𝑦) on dimension 𝑑
311   and meant value 𝑠𝑑 :
312   Manuscript submitted to ACM
      Necessary but Not Sufficient: Preserving the Writer’s Commitments and Covering Their Intended Message in LLM
      Keyword Expansion for AAC                                                                                    7
313
            • Violation. 𝑑 ∈ 𝐶 (𝑥) and 𝐿𝑑 (𝑦) is not admissible for 𝑠𝑑 . The model held the value and did not keep it. This is
314
315
              the only miss a checker can enforce.
316         • Assumption. 𝑑 ∉ 𝐶 (𝑥) and 𝐿𝑑 (𝑦) is a committed value. The model chose a reading the input left open. An
317           assumption is not a pass or a fail; we report its direction and share.
318
            • Loss. 𝑑 ∉ 𝐶 (𝑥) and 𝐿𝑑 (𝑦) ≠ 𝑠𝑑 . The writer’s value never reached the model. Loss and assumption are the same
319
320
              open-channel event seen from the interface (the value was lost) and from the model (it committed anyway); a
321           loss is never charged as a violation.
322
323
324   Because the two variants of a pair share their open-channel input, a model that commits is wrong on exactly one
325   of them, so paired match in an open channel has a ceiling that the design fixes; the quantity of interest is the gap
326
      between open and cue channels, not the open-channel rate. The cue gain (chip minus non-critical) measures how
327
      much of the loss a cue recovers, and the irrelevant-chip control separates cue content from added tokens (Section 4). A
328
329   reference-fidelity score would charge the model for what the interface omitted; this rule does not.
330     Verdicts are computed in code from labels, the item specification and the channel, identically for treated and untreated
331
      outputs. Plain assertion is honoured by emphatic certainty; unresolved critical labels are excluded and counted, not
332
      filled from either extractor prompt; and the added-content field is carried as a descriptive rate and enters no verdict
333
334   rule. Refusal and acceptance speech-act families are quarantined from every primary estimate because their predicate
335   failed the oracle tests of Section 3.4. The rule decides only whether explicit, text-level values are kept. It does not
336
      judge fluency, reference resolution or whether the writer would accept the sentence; the writer-grounded analysis of
337
      Section 5.1 extends the attribution to that question.

        Input honesty (R5). A failure cannot be charged to a model if the displayed input misstated the claim. A validator
341
342   therefore decodes the rendered input and rejects any cue that contradicts the source before generation. Replayed on
343   13,496 chip inputs from the confirmation, composition and hw1 sets, it found 0 conflicts; 10,528 inputs were verified
344
      compatible and 2,968 (time chips and one long alias) lie outside its decoder, with 0 generation or judge calls. Two defects
345
      found while building the harness, a relaxed acceptance predicate and an input-builder fallback, and the regression test
346
347   each produced, are traced in the concurrent archival paper [4].

350   3.3   Extractor, judge pair and arbiter
351
352
      The extractor is gpt-6.1-sol at low reasoning effort, a model that is not among those under test. It sees the output alone,
353   not the source, the model or the channel, except for the added-content question, which also sees the visible input. Two
354   prompt variants, J1 and J2, differ in wording and option order. On hw1 they labelled 12,823 unique output units and
355
      agreed on the critical label for 98.7% (𝜅 = .986); the remaining 170 units (1.3%) went to a blind arbiter. The arbiter is a
356
357
      Claude model from a different family than the judge (claude-opus-5-5 for the confirmation set, claude-sonnet-5-5 for the
358   other runs) and sees only the output text, the critical question and its options. No J1 fallback enters a primary estimate,
359   and every hw1 critical label is resolved by agreement or by the arbiter. In the confirmation set the arbiter resolved
360
      177 units (259 rows) after outcomes were known, blind to reference, model and condition, and the unknown-masked
361
362
      analysis is kept as a sensitivity analysis. One tested model, gpt-6.1-terra, shares a vendor with the judge; its critical-label
363   𝜅 against humans is reported with the other models in Section 3.5.

586
587        Setting. A keyword string that omits a cue leaves the model to choose a reading, and the choice is a default. Whether
588   a default is a good guess depends on how often the omitted cue was present in what the writer meant. We measured that
589

591   in total, of which 8.6% are negated by a detector label), each shortened to keywords by three human keyword sets,

593   politeness, sentence type), the dimensions for which a lexical test of “the keywords carry the cue” exists. The source
594
      cue labels are detector labels; a human check of those labels is a pending human annotation task and, until it returns,
595
596   the prices below carry the detector’s error.
597
598        Affirmative is the right guess for negation; the cost is the asymmetry. Among keyword strings with no negation word,
599   the source was negated in 2.7% [1.8, 3.7] of cases (2.0% by Bayes’ rule with the corpus prevalence; 2.2% in the unweighted
600
      random half). Guessing the affirmative reading is therefore correct for all but a few messages in a hundred, and the
601
      models take it: they output an affirmative sentence for 99.9% of such inputs. The direction of the default is the rational
602
603   one, which is consistent with prior reports that language models state negative content too positively [10]; we do not
604   present the default as new. What the base rate adds is the price. People dropped the negation word from 21.4% [15.4,
605
      27.8] of the negated messages, so an affirmative sentence silently reverses 23.9 [15.5, 32.5] messages per 1,000, while a
606

607
608   reversed negation costs less than 36.4 times a spurious one, and a prompt would have to be shown on about 37.4 inputs
609   per reversal it prevents. The models’ confidence also exceeds the evidence: they assert a negation on 0.1% of these

816
817   If a cue costs a tap, a system could spend taps only where a cue is likely to be missing. We tested ranking rules for

866                      Guard and baselines. The guard (G1) is a monitor on the output plus a bounded retry. The monitor flags an output
867

868

869

       4.9    What These Studies Establish
996
997    The default is measurable, mostly attributable to a missing cue, and a supplied cue removes most of it on the dimensions
998    measured. Against people’s own keyword choices the affirmative default is the right guess for negation and costs about
999
       23.9 silent reversals per 1,000 messages; it is not a prompt artefact and holds in 6 models from 5 families; a one-token
1000
1001   cue or a plain word removes nearly all of the negation and politeness cost on the same items (to 1.6 and 0.7 per 1,000
1002   with a chip); the cue’s content, not its presence, does the work; and a guard with feedback repairs violations that a
1003   no-feedback retry does not, with an inconclusive usability audit (no cost detected under a frozen rule, interval wide) and
1004
       with SLP-weighted failure falling from 22.0% to 14.7% on fresh families. These results answer whether a commitment is
1005
1006   preserved. Whether the preserved draft is the message the writer meant is a different question, which §5.1 takes up
1007   with the writers themselves.

1023

1197   5.3     Event and role structure as a commitment dimension
1198
1199
       The commitment vector used so far has 7 dimensions. The whole-message analysis shows a failure that none of them
1200   names: the draft says something that is fluent, keeps the negation, the speech act and the tone, and nevertheless assigns
1201   the wrong participants to the wrong event. “I am sure I need to wait for the taxi” keeps the certainty chip and changes
1202
       who does what. We propose event and role structure as an additional commitment dimension, defined as the event
1203
1204
       the message describes and the participants that fill its roles (who acts, who or what is acted on, where or for whom),
1205   including the event to which a negation or modal attaches. It differs from the existing agent dimension in scope: agent
1206   covers who performs the stated action, whereas event and role structure covers whether the action, its participants and
1207
       its negation scope are the ones the writer meant. Because the judge labels only the dimension a pair contrasts, a role
1208
1209
       error on an item whose contrast is certainty is not counted.
1210        Three independent sources point to the same dimension. First, substitution is the leading primary reason among
1211   judge-correct rejections (48.8%) and the leading reason among drafts the writer said were not the intended version
1212
       (72.7%). Second, in the SLP panel substitution is a primary reason for 11 of 44 outputs the judge passed and the SLP
1213
1214
       marked partly or no. Third, the design consultants asked for relations to be preserved without being shown this analysis:
1215   one asked that the negation attach to the intended action, the other that the system preserve “who reads, what is
1216   negated, and what the sentence is about” (Section 5.6). Both also rejected drafts in which a button such as TELL became
1217
       a spoken verb that created a new event of telling someone. We state the dimension as a proposal. It has no detector and
1218
1219
       no human-validated rate in this paper, and the substitution label comes from a rule-based diff classifier (agreement with
1220   hand coding 80.0%), not from a role-level annotation.

       5.7   Frontier limits: asking, model uncertainty and partner context
1385
1386   The preceding results suggest several tempting ways to avoid supplying a cue on every message: ask the writer only
1387   when it matters, let the model’s own uncertainty decide, or let the conversation partner’s last remark supply context.
1388
       We tested each in a controlled, machine-labelled study. All labels in this subsection are machine labels (two judges with
1389
1390
       a blind arbiter), the studies are pilot-scale and post-pilot rounds are marked as such in the evidence files, and no human
1391   has yet checked them.
1392
1393      Ask policies. The ask-policy comparison of Section 4.5 (Figure 3) is the first of these tests: no ranking rule, including
1394   a clarification-style trigger, beats asking at random among messages that lack the cue at matched taps, and supplying
1395   the cue on every message (1.6 polarity reversals per 1,000 with a chip, 2.6 with a plain word) undercuts them. A learned
1396
       gate that uses model internals averts a modest share of SLP-weighted speech-act reversals under simulated resolution
1397
1398   rates, but a supervised entropy-feature learner matches it; we treat that study as an evaluation framework and not as a
1399   result.
1400

1402   distribution over the dropped negation, using 2 local models, an untruncated sampler and 540 messages in which the
1403

1415   writer can be asked for the cue.

1417
            Partner context improves defaults and adds an echo. Giving the model the conversation partner’s last remark is a
1418
       published way to improve expansion accuracy [8], and we tested whether it also moves the polarity default. With 130
1419
1420   polarity families, 4 models and model-written partner remarks, a negative remark raises the share of negated bare
1421   output by +36.7 points [+32.6, +40.7], mostly by echoing the remark or agreeing with it. Excluding verbatim echoes
1422
       and agreement replies, the excess is +8.7 points [+6.9, +10.6]; it depends on who wrote the remarks, from +4.2 with one
1423
       remark author to +9.2 with the other on the 40 families written by both. A supplied polarity cue largely removes it: the
1424
1425   excess is +7.1 points [+5.3, +9.0] when echoes and agreements are counted as harm, and +1.2 [+0.2, +2.2] when they
1426   are not. A question remark does not raise the affirmative default; it lowers the affirmative share by -6.0 points through
1427
       abstention. The study is a controlled demonstration with templated remarks. It says nothing about the rate in real AAC
1428
       conversation or about whether echoing a remark the writer agrees with is harmful, and no zero-tap mitigation we tried
1429
1430   beat random asking at matched taps.
1431
1432        What the limits establish. Taken together, these results fix the role of the cue. Asking, model uncertainty and partner
1433   context each reduce reversals only partly or at a tap cost that a cue-on-every-message policy undercuts on the measured
1434
       dimensions, and the cue still has to be elicited from the writer. The remaining problem is not the trigger but covering
1435
1436
       the writer’s whole intended message from a compressed input, which Sections 5.1 and 5.6 show is a question about
1437   approval-gated drafts and not about a better filter.

       6     Discussion
1440
1441   6.1    What the evidence establishes
1442
       Three results carry the paper, and one measurement result qualifies how far they reach. First, the affirmative default is a
1443
1444   property of the model family, not of one prompt: with bare keywords every one of the 6 models (5 families) chooses
1445   the affirmative reading in at least 99.0% of inputs, and without the prompt legend the pooled share is still 98.4% [97.4,
1446   99.2]. We do not present the direction of this bias as new; it builds on the positivity bias that Chen et al. report for
1447
       keyword-to-sentence generation about negative commonsense facts [10], adds the base rate in human-written keyword
1448
1449   messages, and prices the default per dimension. At the human base rate of negation in keyword messages (2.7%), always
1450   committing to the default leaves 23.8 silent polarity reversals per 1,000 messages. Second, a one-token cue removes
1451   most of that cost: with the cue on every message, polarity reversals fall to 1.6 (chip) or 2.6 (plain word) per 1,000,
1452
       politeness reversals from 51.8 to 0.7 and 1.2, and chip plus guard raises speech-act restoration from 75.6% to 90.4%.
1453
1454   An irrelevant control cue does not do this (Section 4). Third, the guard that repairs a failed draft by deletion raises
1455   speech-act restoration on the same items from 75.6% to 90.4% (its transfer to fresh families is restated from our earlier
1456   Manuscript submitted to ACM
       Necessary but Not Sufficient: Preserving the Writer’s Commitments and Covering Their Intended Message in LLM
       Keyword Expansion for AAC                                                                                    29
1457
       paper in Section 4.6), and the blind repair audit was inconclusive on usability: the difference was −13.3 points with
1458
1459
       interval −27.6 to +1.5, which neither shows nor excludes a cost (Section 4.6). Against each repair’s own failed original,
1460   the audit found large gains in preserved meaning (+30.0 points) and fewer invented stances (−52.6 points).
1461     The qualifying result is that these gains concern a floor, not the target. Writers accepted 1 of 200 drafts as written,
1462
       and among drafts whose critical commitment the judge had marked correct, 61.1% were still not the version the writer
1463
1464
       intended (77 of 126). The reasons were compression loss and misresolved events or roles, not added content: in 98% of
1465   the rejected cases the writer’s sentence contained words absent from the keywords, so no model could have recovered
1466   them, and 48.8% of the judge-correct rejections were event or role substitutions (who does what to whom). Added
1467
       content accounted for 0.0% of primary reasons. A cue nevertheless lifts the match to the writer’s intended version from
1468
1469
       11.2% to 39.2% (+28.0 points, interval +14.8 to +42.0, clustered over 20 writers), so the cue is the most effective single
1470   input measured here while remaining far from sufficient. We therefore read critical-commitment preservation as a hard
1471   filter that a draft must pass before it is offered, and the coverage of the writer’s whole intended message, per tap and
1472
       per draft read, as the objective that remains open (Section 6.5).

1475   6.2   Design implications for AAC
1476
       The implications below follow from the measured results and from the requirement changes that two paid design
1477
1478
       consultants who use AAC proposed after reviewing the requirements and stored example outputs (Section 5.6). We
1479   report the consultants’ input as changes to the requirement list; their experiences are not data and the list remains our
1480   proposal. Table 19 states each implication with its evidence and its status. An implication marked measured rests on
1481
       a result in this paper; one marked consultant was proposed by the consultants and is adopted as a requirement; one
1482
1483
       marked untested is a design hypothesis that the studies in Section 6.5 are built to test.
1484
1485     A concrete interface recommendation, and what is unmeasured. Our evidence supports a specific design: show one
1486   draft at a time, with the selected keywords and any cue visible beside it; make the first action “speak the original
1487
       selection” and the second “approve this draft”, never a default; offer the negation and politeness cues as a standing
1488
       control that costs one selection before generation; and offer a second, differing draft only when the cue has been
1489
1490   supplied and the writer rejects the first. We rank this design on meaning restored and acceptance, not on tap or reading
1491   cost: the paper never measured taps, reading time, scanning order or gaze effort, so “lowest cost” is the objective of the
1492
       study that follows and not a result. A cue costs the writer a selection in a direct-touch layout, and may cost far more
1493
       under scanning.
1494
1495     Two points in the table deserve emphasis. The first is the approval gate (I3). A keyword system that speaks its best
1496   sentence converts every residual error into a statement the AAC user did not choose, and the writer-grounded numbers
1497
       show the residual is large even when the critical commitment survives. The consultants framed the gate as control
1498
       over delivery, not as a safety feature: drafting and editing never speak or send, the person decides when a draft is
1499
1500   spoken, and the person may keep an unusual, blunt, short or incomplete sentence. This also constrains the guard. A
1501   deletion-based repair is acceptable as a proposal that the person sees and approves; it is not acceptable as a silent edit of
1502
       what is spoken. The SLPs reflect the same caution from the clinical side: they judged 32.0% of repairs acceptable to
1503
       speak as they stand (40 rater-judgments), against 0.0% of the originals for the same inputs.
1504
1505     The second is the choice between asking and cueing (I5, I7). Ask policies are attractive because a question costs one
1506   turn. Our comparison shows no resolvable advantage of any tested policy over gated-random asking at the same budget,

       6.3   Limitations
1568
1569   We state each limitation once, with the claim it bounds.
1570
1571     Who contributed the human data. Writers, annotators and raters were adult non-author university classmates,
1572   consented and paid (payment and food). They are not AAC users, and the writer-intent results (acceptance, intended-
1573
       version match) are agreement between writers and drafts built from their own keywords, not validation by AAC users.
1574
       We make no claim of benefit to AAC users and no claim about how AAC users would write keywords, which cues they
1575
1576   would use or how they would review drafts. The 2 speech-language pathologists are an expert review of a fixed set of
1577   outputs, not a population estimate, and their agreement statistics describe this pair. The 2 consultants are not a sample
1578
       of AAC users; their feedback shaped the requirements, concerned a text-based design only, and does not establish what
1579
       AAC users in general want.
1580
1581
         Scale and status of the replication. The human-written pair replication has 6 to 7 writers per contrast and is exploratory;
1582
1583
       we claim direction and consistency across writers and models (the chip gain over a non-critical cue is 35.3 points [31.2,
1584   40.3] over 67 families), not interval width. The study is pre-specified and git-timestamped; it is not pre-registered.
1585
1586     The usability audit. The interval includes zero but is wide: the lower bound is −27.6 points, and the repaired texts are
1587   less often usable and preserving together than controls (−20.0 points). Control items are easier, so the gap is confounded
1588
       with item difficulty and we do not read it as a cost of repair. We therefore describe the audit as inconclusive, with the
1589
       interval shown, and not as “no cost” or “as good as controls”. Repairs of the smallest local model are usable in 31.6% of
1590
1591   cases (6 of 19), which bounds where the guard can be recommended. Each item was rated by 3 independent raters, all
1592   classmates.
1593
1594     Measurement of the whole message. The whole-message analysis is post hoc. The reason classifier agrees with a hand
1595   coder at 𝜅 = 0.71 on a 40-item check; because the hand coder is an LLM and the classifier rules were written after
1596
       seeing those items, the figure is optimistic and not held out. Compression loss is also partly a property of keyword
1597
1598
       input: when a writer’s sentence carries content that the keywords never contained, no single draft can be accepted as
1599   written. The acceptance rate (1 of 200) is therefore best read as a ceiling on what the single-draft paradigm can reach,
1600   not as a ranking of the four models rated.
1601
1602     Cost model and frontier. The base rate of negation (2.7% of keyword messages) comes from a posterior over a labelled
1603
       corpus; speech-act and politeness rates rest on detector-proxy labels and are marked provisional. The ask-policy
1604
       comparison is a null of limited power (minimum detectable difference 8.5 per 1,000 for polarity), not an equivalence
1605
1606   result. The reversals-per-1,000 arithmetic assumes the asked person supplies the cue; the same-items measurement on
1607   332 units replaces the earlier transported estimate of that quantity.
1608
1609     Scope of the system studied. All inputs are English text keyword strings; we did not study speech output, access
1610   methods, vocabulary layout, prediction interfaces, multi-turn context or sustained use. Model outputs come from
1611
       specific model versions and a hosted API that does not honour a random seed, so exact outputs are not reproducible

1617   6.4    Ethics and positionality
1618
1619
       Annotators were adult non-author university classmates who gave consent and received payment and food. They
1620   labelled and audited model outputs; some also chose keywords and wrote sentence pairs that we release under CC BY 4.0
1621   with their consent. We collected no personal data, no demographic, health or disability information, and no recordings;
1622
       participants are identified only by an ID, could stop at any time and were paid for the time spent. Two speech-language
1623
1624
       pathologists were paid expert annotators of study materials, working anonymously under a written professional-services
1625   agreement; the data concern the outputs, not the raters. Two paid design consultants who use AAC were engaged who
1626   reviewed requirements and examples under a written consulting agreement. This was design consultation, not research
1627
       participation, and we (the authors) decided that it was; we did not collect, analyse or report their experiences, and we
1628
1629
       report only which requirements changed and why. As independent researchers we did not seek an institutional review
1630   determination, and we make no claim of approval or exemption. A study that recruits AAC users to use or evaluate a
1631   system requires institutional review before recruitment, and none is reported here.
1632
            The main ethical risk this work addresses is also its principal limit. A system that expands a person’s words into
1633
1634
       a fluent sentence takes part in authorship: when it is wrong it does not signal an error, it speaks for the person, and
1635   the first-person voice makes that harm easy to miss. Prior work on AI in AAC records that language models can both
1636   help and hinder communication [22], and our results give a quantified instance: a missing cue silently produces the
1637
       opposite polarity or the wrong sentence type at a measurable rate, and a draft that keeps every critical commitment
1638
1639
       is still rarely the sentence the writer meant. For this reason we argue for approval-gated drafts, exact-copy defaults
1640   and visible differences, and we present the guard as a proposal generator, not an editor of what is spoken. The cue
1641   and guard results are measurements of model behaviour on classmates’ keyword messages; they are not evidence that
1642
       deploying either would benefit a person who relies on AAC, and the paper should not be cited for that. The released
1643
1644
       pairs and stored outputs contain no personal data, but they could be used to tune a system to pass our checks without
1645   improving communication; we state the checks as a floor for this reason.
1646        Positionality. This paper takes up the design of tools for a community that the data collected here do not represent.
1647
       Our consultants and SLPs shaped requirements and severity weights but did not review the analysis or approve the
1648
1649
       claims, and the requirements remain our proposals; we use the terms expert annotator and design consultant and avoid
1650   “co-design” and “participatory” because neither describes what happened.

       6.5    Future work
1653
1654        A study with AAC users. The central open question is whether the results transfer to the people for whom the tools
1655
       are intended. A study with AAC users, under institutional review and with an access-method-aware protocol, would
1656
1657
       test what this paper cannot: which cues users choose to supply and in which route; how often they can detect a wrong
1658   draft in a single draft and in a set; the reading and selection effort for scanning, switch, eye-gaze and direct-touch users;
1659   whether exact-copy and approval gates are used as the consultants expect; and whether users want the system to learn
1660
       preferences from a single approval, which the consultants cautioned against. The consultants also noted that the chip
1661
1662
       and plain-word routes need a working prototype with real navigation and scanning order to compare, because our
1663   evidence on the two routes concerns meaning restored, not effort.

1665
          Intent coverage of approval-gated draft sets. The measure we propose to replace single-draft correctness is coverage
1666
1667
       of the writer’s whole intended message. Given a writer’s own keywords, optional cue and intended sentence, a draft set
1668   covers the item when the writer marks some draft as the intended message or as the intended message with a small
1669   edit; the burden is the number of cue taps, drafts read and selections. Uncovered items are attributed to loss (content
1670
       the keywords could not carry), violation (a commitment the draft got wrong) or assumption (a reading the model
1671
1672
       chose without a basis), using the reason classifier of Section 5.1. The baselines are the single draft (intended-version
1673   match 11.2% bare and 39.2% with a chip; accepted as written 1 of 200), diverse sampling, prompted diversity, and a
1674   random-dimension set that tests whether targeting the contrast, not variety, drives any gain. The pilot design was
1675
       written before any draft set was generated, and its allowed conclusions are declared in advance, including that sets help
1676
1677
       only when the cue is missing, and that they do not help beyond one cue tap; a null is reportable. It will separate loss
1678   that no draft could recover (the keywords do not contain it) from loss that a better set could, which is the ceiling that
1679   Section 5.1 motivates. Writers in the pilot are again classmates rating sets built from their own pairs, so its outcome is
1680
       writer-intent agreement and not validation by AAC users.
1681
1682
1683
          The commitment model and its detectors. The whole-message result asks for an eighth dimension, event and role
1684   structure, that has an annotation scheme, a reliable detector and a human agreement estimate, none of which exist yet;
1685   the seven dimensions measured here are a floor. Prediction-powered inference [1] offers a route to correct machine-judge
1686
       estimates with a small human sample, which we regard as a methods addition, not a replacement for the human arbiter.
1687
1688
1689
          Boundary conditions. Machine-only explorations suggest that a conversation partner’s preceding remark and the
1690   language of the message each move the default; these call for human-labelled checks before any claim. In particular,
1691   whether a synthetic voice carries a contrast that the text carries (for example certainty or politeness) is a separate
1692
       question that no part of this study addresses.
