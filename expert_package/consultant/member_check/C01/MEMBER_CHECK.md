| 1 | Who decides when a draft is spoken | A made-up sentence should stay a draft until the person approves it. The person can hear or look at it privately, change it, or cancel. The person's own words stay available, and can be spoken directly. | We added a new rule, R6. A draft is never spoken or sent without the person's approval, and the typed words stay available. |
| 2 | R1, keep the button's meaning | A chosen button must keep its meaning and its reach. NOT must stay with the action it was meant for. If two choices clash, the system should ask instead of picking one. | R1 now says NOT stays with the intended action, and that a clash between two choices is shown next to those choices. |
| 3 | R2, typed sentences | A complete typed sentence should be spoken as typed, unless the person asks for a rewrite. A rewrite needs a visible proposed change and approval before speech. The bad example under R2 did not show this. | R2 now says exact copy is the default, and a rewrite needs a request and a preview. We did not make a new R2 example for the packet. We wrote down that the old example missed the point. |
| 4 | R3, unclear words | Show the one choice that is unclear, such as who acts or whether it is a question. The person can pick, keep the short words, or cancel. The extra steps should be short and easy to undo. One answer should not quietly become a lasting habit. | R3 now pairs the logged guess with a short choice that can be undone. We have not written a rule about lasting habits. |
| 5 | R4, adding a button | Keep every meaning that was already chosen and still fits. If a new button changes an earlier one on purpose, show the change for approval. TELL means a statement. It must not become the word "tell". | R4 now covers who does what, not only button labels. We added a test: a TELL button must not produce a "tell" verb that the words did not have. |
| 6 | What never to change without asking | Do not change yes or no, who acts, ownership, permission, consent, urgency, numbers, names, event time, or a chosen tone without asking. Do not invent reasons, feelings or promises. A blunt message must not become a polite suggestion. | Partly. We test the things our coding scheme scores. We do not yet test names, amounts, ownership, consent or urgency, and we say so. |
| 7 | Button or plain word | A button is easier to imagine, but this is a design preference, not proof that buttons need fewer steps. Plain words should stay an equal option. Test real steps on a working prototype. The two mockups also differed in event time. | We have not picked a default and we do not say either is easier. Comparing real steps needs a study with users, which we have not done. We did not redo the mockups in the packet. Our tests use the same message for both ways. |
| 8 | The test examples | Check what a test input really says before judging the output. A bad meaning button is a different problem from a system that ignores a good button. | This was already in R5. The two inputs that were wrong became test cases in our checker. |
